> 🎯 **GATE Weightage:** 1–2 marks (Special Topic). Analytic functions are the "well-behaved" complex functions that underpin all of complex analysis. The concept of analyticity is far richer than real differentiability — an analytic function is **infinitely differentiable, expressible as a power series, and has real/imaginary parts that are harmonic**. In aerospace, analytic functions power **potential flow theory** and **conformal mapping** for airfoil analysis.

---

## 📌 Part I: From Differentiability to Analyticity

### Limit & Continuity Review for Complex Functions

For $f(z)$, the limit $\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = L$ means: for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $|f(z) - L| < \varepsilon$ whenever $0 < |z - z_0| < \delta$.

The key difference from real limits: $z \to z_0$ can approach from **any direction** in the complex plane (infinite directions!), and the limit must be the same regardless of direction.

---

### Complex Differentiability

$f(z)$ is **differentiable at $z_0$** if:
$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{\Delta z \to 0} \frac{f(z_0 + \Delta z) - f(z_0)}{\Delta z}$$
exists (same value for ALL paths of approach).

> ⚡ **Critical insight:** This is FAR more restrictive than real differentiability. In the reals, approaching $x_0$ means from left or right (2 directions). In the complex plane, there are **infinitely many** directions — and the limit must agree for ALL of them. This is why analytic functions are so special!

---

### Analytic Function — Formal Definition

$f(z)$ is **analytic (holomorphic/regular)** at $z_0$ if it is **differentiable at $z_0$ AND in some neighborhood of $z_0$**.

$f(z)$ is analytic in domain $D$ if it is analytic at every point of $D$.

**Terminology:**
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Analytic at $z_0$** | Differentiable at $z_0$ and in some open disk around $z_0$ |
| **Entire function** | Analytic at every point of $\mathbb{C}$ |
| **Holomorphic** | Same as analytic (European terminology) |
| **Regular** | Same as analytic (British terminology) |
| **Singular point** | Where $f$ fails to be analytic |

## 🧠 Part II: Cauchy-Riemann Equations — The Master Test

### The Theorem (Necessary Condition)

If $f(z) = u(x,y) + iv(x,y)$ is differentiable at $z_0 = x_0 + iy_0$, then:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}}$$

### The Converse (Sufficient Condition)

If $u_x, u_y, v_x, v_y$ all **exist**, are **continuous**, and satisfy the C-R equations in a neighborhood of $z_0$, then $f$ is differentiable (and hence analytic) at $z_0$.

### The Derivative Formula (When C-R is Satisfied)

$$\boxed{f'(z) = u_x + iv_x = v_y - iu_y}$$

(Four equivalent ways to compute $f'(z)$, all give the same answer when $f$ is analytic.)

---

### C-R Equations: The "Approaching from Two Directions" Derivation

**Approach 1 — Along real axis** ($\Delta z = \Delta x$, $\Delta y = 0$):
$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{[u(x_0+\Delta x, y_0) - u(x_0,y_0)] + i[v(x_0+\Delta x, y_0) - v(x_0,y_0)]}{\Delta x} = u_x + iv_x$$

**Approach 2 — Along imaginary axis** ($\Delta z = i\Delta y$, $\Delta x = 0$):
$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{\Delta y \to 0}\frac{[u(x_0, y_0+\Delta y)-u(x_0,y_0)]+i[v(x_0,y_0+\Delta y)-v(x_0,y_0)]}{i\Delta y} = \frac{u_y + iv_y}{i} = v_y - iu_y$$

Setting the two equal: $u_x + iv_x = v_y - iu_y$

→ Equating real parts: $u_x = v_y$ ✅  
→ Equating imaginary parts: $v_x = -u_y$ ✅

---

### C-R Equations in Polar Form $(z = re^{i\theta})$

If $f(z) = u(r,\theta) + iv(r,\theta)$:
$$\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial v}{\partial \theta}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \boxed{\frac{\partial v}{\partial r} = -\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial u}{\partial \theta}}$$

The derivative in polar form:
$$f'(z) = e^{-i\theta}\!\left(u_r + iv_r\right) = \frac{e^{-i\theta}}{r}(v_\theta - iu_\theta)$$

## 🧠 Part III: Analytic vs Non-Analytic — Pattern Recognition

### Always Analytic (Entire Functions)
| Function | Why |
|---|---|
| Any polynomial $p(z) = a_0 + a_1 z + \cdots + a_n z^n$ | C-R satisfied everywhere |
| $e^z$ | C-R satisfied everywhere; $|e^z| = e^x \ne 0$ |
| $\sin z$, $\cos z$ | Defined via $e^{iz}$, entire |
| $\sinh z$, $\cosh z$ | Defined via $e^z$, entire |

### Analytic Except at Isolated Points
| Function | Singularity |
|---|---|
| $\frac{1}{z}$ | $z = 0$ (simple pole) |
| $\frac{z}{z^2 - 1}$ | $z = \pm 1$ (simple poles) |
| $\frac{1}{(z-i)^3}$ | $z = i$ (pole of order 3) |
| $\tan z$ | $z = (n+\frac{1}{2})\pi$ (simple poles) |

### NEVER Analytic (Fails C-R Everywhere or Almost Everywhere)
| Function | Why |
|---|---|
| $\bar{z} = x - iy$ | $u_x = 1 \ne v_y = -1$ — fails everywhere |
| $|z|^2 = x^2 + y^2$ | $u = x^2+y^2$, $v = 0$: $u_x = 2x$, $v_y = 0$ — fails except at $z=0$ |
| $\text{Re}(z) = x$ | $u_x = 1 \ne v_y = 0$ — fails everywhere |
| $\text{Im}(z) = y$ | Fails everywhere |
| $|z|$ | Not differentiable anywhere |

> ⚡ **GATE Pattern:** Any function involving $\bar{z}$, $|z|$, $\text{Re}(z)$, or $\text{Im}(z)$ explicitly → **NOT analytic** anywhere (except possibly at isolated points).

## 🧠 Part IV: Harmonic Functions & Conjugates (Deep Dive)

### The Laplace Connection

If $f(z) = u + iv$ is analytic in $D$, differentiating the C-R equations:

From $u_x = v_y$: differentiate w.r.t. $x$ → $u_{xx} = v_{yx}$  
From $u_y = -v_x$: differentiate w.r.t. $y$ → $u_{yy} = -v_{xy}$

Adding: $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = v_{yx} - v_{xy} = 0$ (by equality of mixed partials)

$$\boxed{\nabla^2 u = u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0 \qquad \nabla^2 v = v_{xx} + v_{yy} = 0}$$

Both $u$ and $v$ are **harmonic** in $D$. They are called **harmonic conjugates** of each other.

---

### Systematic Procedure: Finding Harmonic Conjugate $v$ Given $u$

**Step 1:** Verify $u$ is harmonic: check $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$.

**Step 2:** Use $\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = u_x$ → integrate w.r.t. $y$ (hold $x$ fixed):
$$v = \int u_x\, dy + \phi(x)$$

**Step 3:** Use $\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -u_y$ → find $\phi'(x)$, then $\phi(x)$.

**Step 4:** Write $v$ and construct $f(z) = u + iv$, then express as a function of $z$ alone.

**Step 5 (Express $f(z)$ directly — the "Substitution Shortcut"):**
After finding $f(z)$ in terms of $x,y$, substitute $x = \frac{z+\bar{z}}{2}$, $y = \frac{z-\bar{z}}{2i}$, OR simply put $y = 0$ (i.e., $x = z$) in the expression for $f = u + iv$:
$$f(z) = [u(z, 0) + iv(z, 0)]\big|_{y=0, x=z}$$

> ⚡ This substitution shortcut works because analytic functions are determined by their values on the real axis!

---

### The Milne-Thomson Method (Direct Route to $f(z)$!)

Given $u(x,y)$, directly find $f(z)$ WITHOUT first finding $v$:

$$\boxed{f(z) = \int \left[u_x(z, 0) - i\, u_y(z, 0)\right] dz + C}$$

where you compute $u_x(x,y)$ and $u_y(x,y)$, then substitute $x = z$, $y = 0$.

**Example:** $u = x^2 - y^2$: $u_x = 2x$, $u_y = -2y$.  
Substitute $x=z, y=0$: $u_x(z,0) = 2z$, $u_y(z,0) = 0$.
$$f(z) = \int (2z - 0)\, dz = z^2 + C \qquad (\text{and indeed } z^2 = x^2-y^2 + 2ixy)$$

## 🧠 Part V: Properties of Analytic Functions (GATE MCQ Facts)

### Property 1: Infinite Differentiability
If $f$ is analytic in $D$, it is infinitely differentiable (has derivatives of all orders) in $D$. This is radically different from real analysis, where a function can be once differentiable but not twice!

### Property 2: Power Series Representation (Taylor's Theorem)
Every analytic function has a convergent power series expansion:
$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(z_0)}{n!}(z-z_0)^n \quad \text{in some disk } |z-z_0| < R$$

The radius $R$ equals the distance to the nearest singularity.

### Property 3: Zeros Are Isolated
If $f$ is analytic and not identically zero, its zeros are **isolated** — no accumulation point of zeros inside the domain of analyticity.

### Property 4: Liouville's Theorem
A **bounded entire function** is constant. That is:
$$|f(z)| \le M \text{ for all } z \in \mathbb{C} \implies f(z) = \text{constant}$$

> ⚡ **Application:** $e^z$ is entire but not bounded (since $|e^z| = e^x \to \infty$ as $x \to \infty$) — consistent with Liouville (since it's not bounded, it needn't be constant).

### Property 5: Maximum Modulus Principle
If $f$ is analytic and non-constant in a domain $D$, then $|f(z)|$ attains its **maximum on the boundary**, never in the interior.

### Property 6: Identity Theorem
If two analytic functions agree on any set with an accumulation point (e.g., a curve, a disk, an infinite sequence converging to a point), they are identical everywhere in the connected domain.

## 🧠 Part VI: Conformal Mapping (Advanced — Aerospace Application)

### Definition
A mapping $w = f(z)$ is **conformal** at $z_0$ if it preserves both **magnitude and sense of angles** between curves.

$$\text{Conformal} \iff f \text{ is analytic at } z_0 \text{ AND } f'(z_0) \ne 0$$

### Why $f'(z_0) \ne 0$?
The argument of $f'(z_0)$ gives the angle of rotation, and $|f'(z_0)|$ gives the scale factor. If $f'(z_0) = 0$ (critical point), the angle-preserving property fails.

### Möbius (Bilinear) Transformation
$$\boxed{w = \frac{az + b}{cz + d}, \quad ad - bc \ne 0}$$

Properties:
- Maps circles and lines → circles and lines
- Is a composition of translations, rotations, scalings, and inversions
- Conformal everywhere except at $z = -d/c$ (simple pole)

### Joukowski Transform (Aerospace Application!)
$$\boxed{w = z + \frac{a^2}{z}}$$

This maps **circles** in the $z$-plane to **airfoil shapes** in the $w$-plane! It's the foundation of thin-airfoil theory and conformal mapping in aerodynamics:
- Circle of radius $a$ centered at origin → line segment (flat plate airfoil)
- Circles slightly off-center → Joukowski airfoil profiles

## ⚡ AIR-1 Speed Tricks & Mnemonics

### 🚀 Trick 1: Three-Step Analyticity Test (30 seconds or less)
1. **Compute $u_x, u_y, v_x, v_y$** (partial derivatives of real/imaginary parts)
2. **Check $u_x = v_y$** and **$u_y = -v_x$**
3. If both hold and partials are continuous → **Analytic** ✅; if either fails → **Not analytic** ❌

---

### 🚀 Trick 2: "Explicit $\bar{z}$ → Not Analytic" Instant Detector
If $f(z)$ is written with $\bar{z}$, $|z|$, $x$, $y$, $\text{Re}(z)$, or $\text{Im}(z)$ explicitly:
- **Automatic fail** (almost certainly not analytic — verify with C-R to be rigorous, but 99% of the time it fails)
- Exception: might be analytic at isolated points (e.g., $|z|^2$ is differentiable only at $z=0$)

---

### 🚀 Trick 3: Milne-Thomson as the FASTEST Path to $f(z)$
When asked "find $f(z)$ given $u(x,y)$" — skip finding $v$ by hand:
1. Compute $u_x(x,y)$ and $u_y(x,y)$
2. Put $x = z$, $y = 0$: get $\phi_1(z) = u_x(z,0)$ and $\phi_2(z) = u_y(z,0)$
3. $f(z) = \int[\phi_1(z) - i\,\phi_2(z)]\,dz + C$

This reduces a complex conjugate-finding problem to a single integration!

---

### 🚀 Trick 4: Radius of Convergence = Distance to Nearest Singularity
For a Taylor series of $f(z)$ at $z_0$:
$$R = \text{distance from } z_0 \text{ to the nearest singularity of } f$$

**Example:** For $f(z) = \frac{1}{1+z^2}$, singularities at $z = \pm i$.  
Taylor series at $z_0 = 0$: $R = |0 - i| = 1$.  
This explains why $\sum(-1)^n x^{2n}$ converges only for $|x| < 1$ in real analysis!

---

### 🧠 Mnemonic: "**CHAIN**" for Properties of Analytic Functions

| Letter | Property |
|---|---|
| **C**-R satisfied | Necessary and sufficient for analyticity |
| **H**armonic | Both $u$ and $v$ satisfy Laplace's equation |
| **A**ll derivatives exist | Infinite differentiability |
| **I**solated zeros | Zeros cannot accumulate inside domain |
| **N**o bounded non-constant | Liouville: bounded entire → constant |

---

### 🧠 Mnemonic: "**REAL functions are analytic if they have NO $\bar{z}$**"
- Functions of $z$ alone (like $z^2, e^z, \sin z$) → analytic
- Functions involving $\bar{z}$ or $|z|$ explicitly → NOT analytic

## 📝 Step-by-Step Solved GATE Examples

### Example 1: Full C-R Analysis (GATE 4-Marker)
**Problem:** Determine where $f(z) = x^3 + 3xy^2 - 3x + i(y^3 + 3x^2y - 3y)$ is analytic.

**Solution:**
$u = x^3 + 3xy^2 - 3x$, $v = y^3 + 3x^2y - 3y$

$$u_x = 3x^2 + 3y^2 - 3, \qquad v_y = 3y^2 + 3x^2 - 3 \quad \implies u_x = v_y \checkmark$$
$$u_y = 6xy, \qquad v_x = 6xy \quad \implies u_y = -v_x \text{ only if } 6xy = -6xy \implies xy = 0$$

C-R fails unless $xy = 0$ (i.e., on the real or imaginary axis). So $f$ is NOT analytic anywhere (it's differentiable only on the axes, not in any open neighborhood).

$$\boxed{f(z) \text{ is nowhere analytic.}}$$

---

### Example 2: Milne-Thomson Method (GATE 3-Marker)
**Problem:** If $u = e^x\cos y$, find $f(z) = u + iv$.

**Solution:**
$u_x = e^x\cos y$, $u_y = -e^x\sin y$.

Milne-Thomson: put $x = z$, $y = 0$:
$$u_x(z, 0) = e^z\cos 0 = e^z, \qquad u_y(z, 0) = -e^z\sin 0 = 0$$

$$f(z) = \int(e^z - i \cdot 0)\,dz = e^z + C$$

**Verification:** $e^z = e^x(\cos y + i\sin y) = e^x\cos y + ie^x\sin y \implies u = e^x\cos y$ ✅, $v = e^x\sin y$ ✅.

$$\boxed{f(z) = e^z + C}$$

---

### Example 3: Maximum Modulus Principle Application (GATE MCQ)
**Problem:** If $f(z)$ is analytic in the disk $|z| \le 1$ with $f(0) = 0$ and $|f(z)| \le 1$ on $|z| = 1$, what is the maximum of $|f(z)|$ inside $|z| < 1$?

**Solution:** By the **Maximum Modulus Principle**, $|f(z)|$ achieves its maximum on the boundary $|z| = 1$.

$$\boxed{\max_{|z| \le 1}|f(z)| = \max_{|z| = 1}|f(z)| \le 1}$$

The maximum is achieved on the boundary, never strictly in the interior (unless $f$ is constant).

---

### Example 4: Radius of Convergence (GATE 1-Marker)
**Problem:** What is the radius of convergence of the Taylor series of $f(z) = \frac{1}{(z-2)(z+3i)}$ about $z_0 = 0$?

**Solution:**
Singularities: $z = 2$ and $z = -3i$.

Distances from $z_0 = 0$:
- $|0 - 2| = 2$
- $|0 - (-3i)| = 3$

Radius = distance to **nearest** singularity = $\min(2, 3) = 2$.

$$\boxed{R = 2}$$

---

### Example 5: Finding $f(z)$ from $u$ by Integration (Standard Method)
**Problem:** Given $u = x^2 - y^2 + 2x$, find $f(z)$ analytic with $f(0) = 1$.

**Solution:**
$u_x = 2x + 2$, $u_y = -2y$.

Milne-Thomson: put $x=z, y=0$:
$$f(z) = \int[(2z+2) - i(0)]\,dz = z^2 + 2z + C$$

At $z = 0$: $f(0) = 0 + 0 + C = 1 \implies C = 1$.

$$\boxed{f(z) = z^2 + 2z + 1 = (z+1)^2}$$

## 📐 Master Summary: Analytic Functions

| Concept | Key Formula/Result |
|---|---|
| **Definition** | Differentiable at $z_0$ AND in neighborhood of $z_0$ |
| **C-R (Cartesian)** | $u_x = v_y$, $u_y = -v_x$ |
| **C-R (Polar)** | $u_r = v_\theta/r$, $v_r = -u_\theta/r$ |
| **Derivative** | $f'(z) = u_x + iv_x = v_y - iu_y$ |
| **Harmonic** | $\nabla^2 u = 0$, $\nabla^2 v = 0$ |
| **Milne-Thomson** | $f(z) = \int[u_x(z,0) - iu_y(z,0)]\,dz + C$ |
| **Power series** | $f(z) = \sum \frac{f^{(n)}(z_0)}{n!}(z-z_0)^n$, $R$ = dist to nearest singularity |
| **Liouville** | Bounded + entire → constant |
| **Max. modulus** | $\max|f|$ on boundary, never interior |
| **Conformal** | Analytic + $f'(z_0) \ne 0$ |
| **Joukowski** | $w = z + a^2/z$ maps circles → airfoils |

## ⚠️ Common Exam Traps & Pitfalls

> 🔴 **Trap 1: Differentiable at a Point ≠ Analytic at That Point**  
> $|z|^2 = x^2 + y^2$: C-R gives $u_x = 2x = v_y = 0$ and $u_y = 2y = -v_x = 0$, satisfied ONLY at $x = y = 0$ (i.e., $z = 0$). So $|z|^2$ is **differentiable at $z = 0$** but **NOT analytic at $z = 0$** (because it's not differentiable in any neighborhood of $0$). Differentiable at a point ≠ analytic at that point!

> 🔴 **Trap 2: C-R Necessary but Not Sufficient Alone**  
> C-R equations are necessary for differentiability, but not sufficient unless the partial derivatives are also **continuous**. A pathological function can satisfy C-R at a point without being differentiable there. GATE always implies continuous partials, but be aware of this subtlety.

> 🔴 **Trap 3: $f(z) = z^n$ vs $f(z) = \bar{z}^n$**  
> $f(z) = z^n$ is entire (analytic everywhere). $f(z) = \bar{z}^n$ is nowhere analytic (for $n \ge 1$). This is a standard 1-marker trap!

> 🔴 **Trap 4: Harmonic Does Not Mean Analytic Part**  
> If $u$ is harmonic, it doesn't mean $u$ IS an analytic function — it means $u$ could be the REAL PART of one. The function $f = u + iv$ (with harmonic conjugate $v$) is analytic, not $u$ alone.
