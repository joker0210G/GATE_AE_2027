> 🎯 **GATE Weightage:** 1–2 marks per paper. The **Normal (Gaussian) Distribution** is the single most important continuous probability distribution in engineering mathematics, aerodynamics, structural reliability, and measurement error analysis. GATE questions heavily test $Z$-score transformations, 68-95-99.7 empirical rules, linear combinations of normal variables (e.g. Margin of Safety = Strength − Stress), and Central Limit Theorem applications!

---

## 📌 Part I: Probability Density Function (PDF) & Parameters

A continuous random variable $X$ follows a **Normal (Gaussian) Distribution** with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, denoted $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, if its PDF is:

$$\boxed{f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad -\infty < x < \infty}$$

where:
- $\mu \in (-\infty, \infty)$: **Mean / Location parameter** (shifts the peak horizontally)
- $\sigma > 0$: **Standard Deviation / Scale parameter** (controls width/spread of the curve)
- $\sigma^2$: Variance
- $\pi \approx 3.14159$, $e \approx 2.71828$

### Total Area Condition
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) \, dx = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} dx = 1$$

## 🧠 Part II: Key Geometric & Statistical Properties of the Bell Curve

$$\begin{array}{|c|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Property} & \textbf{Mathematical Representation / Description} \\ \hline
\textbf{Symmetry} & \text{Perfectly symmetric about } x = \mu. \; f(\mu + h) = f(\mu - h). \\ \hline
\textbf{Central Tendency} & \mathbf{\text{Mean} = \text{Median} = \text{Mode} = \mu}. \text{ Peak occurs at } x = \mu. \\ \hline
\textbf{Peak Height} & f(\mu) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \approx \frac{0.3989}{\sigma}. \\ \hline
\textbf{Points of Inflection} & x = \mu - \sigma \quad \text{and} \quad x = \mu + \sigma \quad \left(\text{where } \frac{d^2f}{dx^2} = 0\right). \\ \hline
\textbf{Asymptote} & \text{The curve approaches } y=0 \text{ as } x \to \pm \infty \text{ (touches at infinity)}. \\ \hline
\textbf{Skewness \& Kurtosis} & \text{Skewness } \gamma_1 = 0 \quad \text{(No skew)}; \quad \text{Kurtosis } \beta_2 = 3 \quad \text{(Mesokurtic)}. \\ \hline
\end{array}$$

## 🧠 Part III: Standard Normal Distribution $Z \sim N(0, 1)$

To calculate probabilities for ANY normal variable $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, we transform it to the **Standard Normal Variable** $Z$:

$$\boxed{Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim N(0, 1)}$$

### Standard Normal PDF $\phi(z)$
$$\phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2}$$

### Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) $\Phi(z)$
$$\boxed{\Phi(z) = P(Z \le z) = \int_{-\infty}^{z} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2} \, dt}$$

---

### Master Rules for Computing Probabilities using $\Phi(z)$

$$\begin{array}{|c|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Formula} \\ \hline
\textbf{Total Area at Mean} & \Phi(0) = P(Z \le 0) = 0.5000 \\ \hline
\textbf{Symmetry Property} & \Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z) \\ \hline
\textbf{Upper Tail Probability} & P(Z > z) = 1 - \Phi(z) = \Phi(-z) \\ \hline
\textbf{Interval Probability} & P(a \le X \le b) = P\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma} \le Z \le \frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right) = \Phi(z_2) - \Phi(z_1) \\ \hline
\textbf{Symmetric Interval} & P(-z_0 \le Z \le z_0) = 2\Phi(z_0) - 1 \\ \hline
\end{array}$$

## 🧠 Part IV: The Empirical 68-95-99.7 Rule (HIGH YIELD!)

For any normal distribution $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$:

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}
P(\mu - 1\sigma \le X \le \mu + 1\sigma) &= P(-1 \le Z \le 1) \approx \mathbf{68.27\%} \quad (0.6827) \\
P(\mu - 2\sigma \le X \le \mu + 2\sigma) &= P(-2 \le Z \le 2) \approx \mathbf{95.45\%} \quad (0.9545) \\
P(\mu - 3\sigma \le X \le \mu + 3\sigma) &= P(-3 \le Z \le 3) \approx \mathbf{99.73\%} \quad (0.9973)
\end{aligned}}$$

### Useful Tail Probabilities (Memorize for Speed!)

| Range | Probability inside | Tail Probability outside (two-sided) | One-sided Tail |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mu \pm 1\sigma$ | $68.27\%$ | $31.73\%$ | $15.87\%$ |
| $\mu \pm 2\sigma$ | $95.45\%$ | $4.55\%$ | $2.28\%$ |
| $\mu \pm 3\sigma$ | $99.73\%$ | $0.27\%$ | $0.135\%$ |

### Critical $Z$-values for Standard Confidence Levels

| Confidence Level | Two-tailed $Z$-critical value | One-tailed $Z$-critical value |
|---|---|---|
| **90%** | $z_{0.05} = \mathbf{1.645}$ | $z_{0.10} = \mathbf{1.282}$ |
| **95%** | $z_{0.025} = \mathbf{1.960}$ | $z_{0.05} = \mathbf{1.645}$ |
| **99%** | $z_{0.005} = \mathbf{2.576}$ | $z_{0.01} = \mathbf{2.326}$ |

## 🧠 Part V: Linear Combination & Additivity Property (CORE GATE APPLICATION!)

If $X_1 \sim N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $X_2 \sim N(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ are **independent** normal random variables:

### 1. General Linear Combination
$$\boxed{W = a X_1 + b X_2 + c \sim N\left(a\mu_1 + b\mu_2 + c, \; a^2\sigma_1^2 + b^2\sigma_2^2\right)}$$

### 2. Sum of Normal Variables
$$\boxed{X_1 + X_2 \sim N\left(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \; \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2\right)}$$

### 3. Difference of Normal Variables (Aerospace Reliability / Structural Margin!)
$$\boxed{X_1 - X_2 \sim N\left(\mu_1 - \mu_2, \; \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2\right)}$$

> 🔴 **GOLDEN GATE RULE:**
> For the difference $X_1 - X_2$, the means **subtract** $(\mu_1 - \mu_2)$, but the **variances ALWAYS ADD** $(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$!
> $$\text{Variance of } (X_1 - X_2) = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 \ne \sigma_1^2 - \sigma_2^2$$

## 🧠 Part VI: Sample Mean Distribution & Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

### 1. Sample Mean of Normal Variables
If $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ is a random sample of size $n$ drawn from $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$:

$$\boxed{\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \sim N\left(\mu, \; \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)}$$

$$Z_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0, 1)$$

---

### 2. Central Limit Theorem (CLT) — The Workhorse of Statistics

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. random variables from **ANY distribution** with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$.

As $n \to \infty$ (practically $n \ge 30$), the distribution of the sample mean $\bar{X}$ approaches a Normal distribution:

$$\boxed{\bar{X} \approx N\left(\mu, \; \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \quad \text{or} \quad S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i \approx N(n\mu, \; n\sigma^2)}$$

$$\boxed{Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \approx N(0, 1)}$$

> ⚡ **Significance:** CLT allows us to use normal distribution formulas for sample averages even if the underlying population is skewed or non-normal!

## 🧠 Part VII: Approximations to Normal Distribution

### 1. Normal Approximation to Binomial $B(n, p)$
When $np \ge 5$ and $n(1-p) \ge 5$:

$$X \sim B(n, p) \approx N(\mu = np, \, \sigma^2 = npq)$$

**Continuity Correction:**
$$P(a \le X \le b) \approx P\left(\frac{a - 0.5 - np}{\sqrt{npq}} \le Z \le \frac{b + 0.5 - np}{\sqrt{npq}}\right)$$

---

### 2. Normal Approximation to Poisson $(\lambda)$
When $\lambda > 15$:

$$X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda) \approx N(\mu = \lambda, \, \sigma^2 = \lambda)$$

## ⚡ AIR-1 Speed Tricks & Mnemonics

### 🚀 Trick 1: Structural Reliability / Interference Calculation
When finding probability of failure $P(\text{Strength } S < \text{Load } L)$ or $P(S - L < 0)$:
1. Define $D = S - L$.
2. $\mu_D = \mu_S - \mu_L$
3. $\sigma_D = \sqrt{\sigma_S^2 + \sigma_L^2}$
4. $Z = \frac{0 - \mu_D}{\sigma_D} = -\frac{\mu_S - \mu_L}{\sqrt{\sigma_S^2 + \sigma_L^2}}$
5. $P(\text{Failure}) = \Phi(Z)$.

---

### 🚀 Trick 2: Symmetry Shortcut for $\Phi(z)$
- $P(Z > 1.96) = 1 - \Phi(1.96) = 1 - 0.975 = 0.025$
- $P(Z < -1.96) = \Phi(-1.96) = 1 - \Phi(1.96) = 0.025$
- $P(|Z| > 1.96) = 2 \times 0.025 = 0.05$ (5% two-tailed)

---

### 🚀 Trick 3: Standard Error vs. Standard Deviation
- Standard Deviation of individual $X$: $\sigma$
- Standard Error of sample mean $\bar{X}$: $\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$
- Standard Deviation of sum $\sum X_i$: $\sigma_{sum} = \sqrt{n}\sigma$

---

### 🧠 Mnemonic: "**BELL-S**" for Normal Curve Features
- **B**ell-shaped & symmetric about $\mu$
- **E**qual Mean, Median, and Mode ($\mu$)
- **L**ocation parameter $\mu$, scale parameter $\sigma$
- **L**imits from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$
- **S**igmas: Inflection points at $\mu \pm 1\sigma$

---

### 🧠 Mnemonic: "**EMPIRIC-68-95-99**"
- $1\sigma \implies 68\%$
- $2\sigma \implies 95\%$
- $3\sigma \implies 99.7\%$

## 📝 Step-by-Step Solved GATE Examples

### Example 1: Basic $Z$-Score Probability (GATE 1-Marker)
**Problem:** The diameter of a shaft produced in a factory follows a normal distribution with mean $\mu = 20\text{ mm}$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 0.1\text{ mm}$. Find the probability that a shaft has a diameter between $19.9\text{ mm}$ and $20.2\text{ mm}$. (Given: $\Phi(1) = 0.8413$, $\Phi(2) = 0.9772$).

**Solution:**
Transform to $Z$-scores:
$$z_1 = \frac{19.9 - 20}{0.1} = \frac{-0.1}{0.1} = -1$$
$$z_2 = \frac{20.2 - 20}{0.1} = \frac{0.2}{0.1} = 2$$

$$P(19.9 \le X \le 20.2) = P(-1 \le Z \le 2) = \Phi(2) - \Phi(-1)$$

Using symmetry $\Phi(-1) = 1 - \Phi(1) = 1 - 0.8413 = 0.1587$:
$$P(-1 \le Z \le 2) = 0.9772 - 0.1587 = \mathbf{0.8185}$$

$$\boxed{P(19.9 \le X \le 20.2) = 0.8185 \quad (81.85\%)}$$

---

### Example 2: Structural Reliability / Load-Strength Margin (GATE 2-Marker)
**Problem:** The tensile strength $S$ of a structural component is normally distributed with mean $400\text{ MPa}$ and SD $30\text{ MPa}$. The applied stress $L$ is independently normally distributed with mean $300\text{ MPa}$ and SD $40\text{ MPa}$. Find the probability of failure $P(S < L)$. (Given: $\Phi(-2) = 0.0228$).

**Solution:**
Define Margin of Safety $M = S - L$.
Since $S$ and $L$ are independent normal variables, $M$ is also normal:
$$\mu_M = \mu_S - \mu_L = 400 - 300 = 100\text{ MPa}$$
$$\sigma_M^2 = \sigma_S^2 + \sigma_L^2 = 30^2 + 40^2 = 900 + 1600 = 2500 \implies \sigma_M = \sqrt{2500} = 50\text{ MPa}$$

Failure occurs when $S < L \iff M < 0$:
$$z = \frac{0 - \mu_M}{\sigma_M} = \frac{0 - 100}{50} = -2$$

$$P(\text{Failure}) = P(M < 0) = P(Z < -2) = \Phi(-2) = \mathbf{0.0228}$$

$$\boxed{P(\text{Failure}) = 0.0228 \quad (2.28\%)}$$

---

### Example 3: Finding $\mu$ and $\sigma$ from Percentages (GATE 2-Marker)
**Problem:** In a normal distribution, $10\%$ of items are above $60$ and $20\%$ are below $30$. Find $\mu$ and $\sigma$. (Given: $P(Z > 1.28) = 0.10 \implies z_{0.90} = 1.28$; $P(Z < -0.84) = 0.20 \implies z_{0.20} = -0.84$).

**Solution:**
**Equation 1:** $P(X > 60) = 0.10 \implies \frac{60 - \mu}{\sigma} = 1.28 \implies 60 - \mu = 1.28\sigma$  
$$\mu + 1.28\sigma = 60 \quad \text{--- (1)}$$

**Equation 2:** $P(X < 30) = 0.20 \implies \frac{30 - \mu}{\sigma} = -0.84 \implies 30 - \mu = -0.84\sigma$  
$$\mu - 0.84\sigma = 30 \quad \text{--- (2)}$$

Subtracting (2) from (1):
$$(1.28 - (-0.84))\sigma = 60 - 30 \implies 2.12 \sigma = 30 \implies \sigma = \frac{30}{2.12} \approx \mathbf{14.15}$$

Substituting $\sigma = 14.15$ into (1):
$$\mu + 1.28(14.15) = 60 \implies \mu + 18.11 = 60 \implies \mu = \mathbf{41.89}$$

$$\boxed{\mu \approx 41.89, \quad \sigma \approx 14.15}$$

---

### Example 4: Central Limit Theorem Application (GATE 2-Marker)
**Problem:** The weight of rivets has mean $\mu = 5\text{ g}$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 0.8\text{ g}$. A package contains 64 rivets. Find the probability that the total weight of the package exceeds $326.4\text{ g}$. (Given: $\Phi(1) = 0.8413$).

**Solution:**
Let $S_{64} = \sum_{i=1}^{64} X_i$ be the total weight of $n = 64$ rivets.
By CLT:
$$E[S_{64}] = n\mu = 64 \times 5 = 320\text{ g}$$
$$Var(S_{64}) = n\sigma^2 = 64 \times (0.8)^2 = 64 \times 0.64 = 40.96 \implies \sigma_{sum} = \sqrt{40.96} = 6.4\text{ g}$$

We want $P(S_{64} > 326.4)$:
$$z = \frac{326.4 - 320}{6.4} = \frac{6.4}{6.4} = 1$$

$$P(S_{64} > 326.4) = P(Z > 1) = 1 - \Phi(1) = 1 - 0.8413 = \mathbf{0.1587}$$

$$\boxed{P(S_{64} > 326.4) = 0.1587 \quad (15.87\%)}$$

---

### Example 5: Linear Combination of Three Variables (GATE 2-Marker)
**Problem:** $X_1 \sim N(10, 4)$, $X_2 \sim N(20, 9)$, and $X_3 \sim N(5, 1)$ are independent. Find the variance of $Y = 2X_1 - 3X_2 + 4X_3 - 7$.

**Solution:**
For linear combination $Y = \sum a_i X_i + c$:
$$\mu_Y = 2(10) - 3(20) + 4(5) - 7 = 20 - 60 + 20 - 7 = -27$$

Variance formula for independent variables:
$$Var(Y) = a_1^2 Var(X_1) + a_2^2 Var(X_2) + a_3^2 Var(X_3) + 0$$
$$Var(Y) = (2)^2 (4) + (-3)^2 (9) + (4)^2 (1) = 4(4) + 9(9) + 16(1)$$
$$Var(Y) = 16 + 81 + 16 = \mathbf{113}$$

$$\boxed{Var(Y) = 113}$$

| Feature / Formula | Expression |
|---|---|
| **PDF** | $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$ |
| **Z-Score** | $Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim N(0, 1)$ |
| **Mean, Median, Mode** | All equal to $\mu$ |
| **Variance & SD** | $Var(X) = \sigma^2, \quad SD(X) = \sigma$ |
| **Points of Inflection** | $x = \mu \pm \sigma$ |
| **Symmetry of CDF** | $\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)$ |
| **Empirical Rule** | $1\sigma \to 68.27\%, \quad 2\sigma \to 95.45\%, \quad 3\sigma \to 99.73\%$ |

## ⚠️ Common Exam Traps & Pitfalls

> 🔴 **Trap 1: Subtracting Variances for $(X_1 - X_2)$**  
> **THE #1 ERROR IN GATE!** When computing $Var(X_1 - X_2)$, students write $\sigma_1^2 - \sigma_2^2$. Variances **ALWAYS ADD** for independent variables: $Var(X_1 - X_2) = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2$.

> 🔴 **Trap 2: Confusing Variance and Standard Deviation in $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ notation**  
> If $X \sim N(10, 25)$, then $\mu = 10$, $\sigma^2 = 25$, so $\sigma = 5$ (NOT $25$!). Always check if the second number given is $\sigma$ or $\sigma^2$.

> 🔴 **Trap 3: Using $\sigma$ instead of $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ for Sample Means**  
> When finding probabilities for a **sample mean** $\bar{X}$, the denominator in the $Z$-score is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ (Standard Error), NOT $\sigma$. Using $\sigma$ yields an incorrect $Z$-score.

> 🔴 **Trap 4: Forgetting Continuity Correction for Discrete Approximations**  
> When approximating $B(n, p)$ or $\text{Poisson}(\lambda)$ with a Normal distribution: $P(X \le k)$ becomes $P(X \le k + 0.5)$ and $P(X \ge k)$ becomes $P(X \ge k - 0.5)$.
