> 🎯 **GATE Weightage:** C-R equations appear in 1–2 questions per paper as direct MCQs. They are the **single most important test** for analyticity of complex functions. This note is your complete C-R reference — derivation, all forms, all consequences, all GATE patterns, zero to AIR-1.

---

## 📌 Part I: Setup — What Are We Testing?

### The Central Question
Given $f(z) = u(x,y) + iv(x,y)$, is $f$ **analytic** (complex differentiable in a neighborhood)?

### The Answer: Cauchy-Riemann (C-R) Equations
A complex function $f = u + iv$ is analytic at a point if and only if its real and imaginary parts satisfy:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}} \qquad \text{AND} \qquad \boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}}$$

This is the **Cauchy-Riemann (C-R) system** — named after Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Bernhard Riemann.

---

## 🧠 Part II: Rigorous Derivation from First Principles

### Setup
Let $f(z) = u(x,y) + iv(x,y)$. For $f$ to be differentiable at $z_0 = x_0 + iy_0$:

$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{\Delta z \to 0} \frac{f(z_0 + \Delta z) - f(z_0)}{\Delta z}$$

must exist and be the **same for every path** of approach $\Delta z \to 0$.

Write $\Delta z = \Delta x + i\Delta y$, so:
$$\frac{f(z_0 + \Delta z) - f(z_0)}{\Delta z} = \frac{[u(x_0+\Delta x, y_0+\Delta y) - u(x_0,y_0)] + i[v(x_0+\Delta x, y_0+\Delta y) - v(x_0,y_0)]}{\Delta x + i\Delta y}$$

### Path 1: Approach along Real Axis ($\Delta y = 0$, $\Delta z = \Delta x \to 0$)

$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{\Delta u + i\Delta v}{\Delta x} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + i\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = u_x + iv_x$$

### Path 2: Approach along Imaginary Axis ($\Delta x = 0$, $\Delta z = i\Delta y \to 0$)

$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{\Delta y \to 0}\frac{\Delta u + i\Delta v}{i\Delta y} = \frac{u_y + iv_y}{i} = \frac{u_y}{i} + v_y = -iu_y + v_y = v_y - iu_y$$

### Setting the Two Results Equal

$$u_x + iv_x = v_y - iu_y$$

Equating **real parts**: $\quad \boxed{u_x = v_y}$

Equating **imaginary parts**: $\quad \boxed{v_x = -u_y} \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \boxed{u_y = -v_x}$

> ⚡ **The Geometric Insight:** The derivative must be the same from ALL directions. Testing just the real and imaginary axis directions gives C-R as a **necessary** condition. Continuity of partial derivatives makes it **sufficient**.

---

## 🧠 Part III: The Four Equivalent Expressions for $f'(z)$

When C-R is satisfied, the derivative can be computed in any of these four equivalent ways:

$$\boxed{f'(z) = u_x + iv_x}$$

$$\boxed{f'(z) = v_y - iu_y}$$

$$\boxed{f'(z) = u_x - iu_y}$$   (since $u_x = v_y$)

$$\boxed{f'(z) = v_y + iv_x}$$   (since $u_y = -v_x$)

> ⚡ **GATE Strategy:** Choose whichever form requires the least computation. In GATE, $f'(z) = u_x + iv_x$ is the most commonly used.

---

## 🧠 Part IV: C-R Equations in Polar Form

When $z = re^{i\theta}$ and $f(z) = u(r,\theta) + iv(r,\theta)$:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial v}{\partial \theta}} \qquad \text{AND} \qquad \boxed{\frac{\partial v}{\partial r} = -\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial u}{\partial \theta}}$$

Or equivalently:
$$\boxed{r\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial \theta}} \qquad \text{AND} \qquad \boxed{r\frac{\partial v}{\partial r} = -\frac{\partial u}{\partial \theta}}$$

**The derivative in polar form:**
$$f'(z) = e^{-i\theta}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} + i\frac{\partial v}{\partial r}\right)$$

---

## 🧠 Part V: Necessary vs. Sufficient Conditions

### Full Analyticity Theorem (Complete Version)

Let $f(z) = u(x,y) + iv(x,y)$.

| Condition | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| $f$ differentiable at $z_0$ | C-R equations hold at $z_0$ (**necessary**) |
| C-R holds at $z_0$ + partials exist at $z_0$ | NOT sufficient for differentiability (partials may be discontinuous) |
| C-R holds at $z_0$ + **all 4 partials continuous** at $z_0$ | $f$ is differentiable at $z_0$ (**sufficient**) |
| C-R + continuous partials in a **neighborhood** | $f$ is **analytic** at $z_0$ |

> ⚡ **Key Point for GATE:** In 99% of GATE problems, the functions given have continuous partial derivatives, so "C-R satisfied" ↔ "analytic." The exception ($|z|^2$, satisfies C-R only at origin but not analytic) is a standard trap question.

## 🧠 Part VI: Comprehensive Pattern Table (GATE's Most Tested Functions)

### Category A: Always Analytic (C-R holds everywhere, continuous partials)

| Function | $u$ | $v$ | $u_x$ | $v_y$ | $u_y$ | $-v_x$ | Analytic? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $z^2$ | $x^2-y^2$ | $2xy$ | $2x$ | $2x$ ✅ | $-2y$ | $-2y$ ✅ | ✅ Entire |
| $z^3$ | $x^3-3xy^2$ | $3x^2y-y^3$ | $3x^2-3y^2$ | $3x^2-3y^2$ ✅ | $-6xy$ | $-6xy$ ✅ | ✅ Entire |
| $e^z$ | $e^x\cos y$ | $e^x\sin y$ | $e^x\cos y$ | $e^x\cos y$ ✅ | $-e^x\sin y$ | $-e^x\sin y$ ✅ | ✅ Entire |
| $\sin z$ | $\sin x\cosh y$ | $\cos x\sinh y$ | $\cos x\cosh y$ | $\cos x\cosh y$ ✅ | $\sin x\sinh y$ | $\sin x\sinh y$ ✅ | ✅ Entire |

### Category B: Never Analytic (C-R fails everywhere)

| Function | $u$ | $v$ | $u_x$ | $v_y$ | C-R? | Analytic? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\bar{z}$ | $x$ | $-y$ | $1$ | $-1$ | ❌ $1\ne-1$ | ❌ Nowhere |
| $\text{Re}(z)$ | $x$ | $0$ | $1$ | $0$ | ❌ $1\ne 0$ | ❌ Nowhere |
| $\text{Im}(z)$ | $y$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ ✅ but $u_y=1\ne-v_x=0$ | ❌ | ❌ Nowhere |
| $|z|$ | $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ | $0$ | $\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}$ | $0$ | ❌ | ❌ Nowhere |

### Category C: Analytic Only at Isolated Points (Classic Trap!)

| Function | C-R Satisfied Where? | Analytic? |
|---|---|---|
| $|z|^2 = x^2+y^2$ | Only at $z=0$ (since $u_x=2x=v_y=0$ only when $x=y=0$) | ❌ Not analytic anywhere (no neighborhood) |
| $f(z) = \bar{z}^2$ | Only at $z=0$ | ❌ Not analytic |

## 🧠 Part VII: The Four Major Consequences of C-R

### Consequence 1: Harmonic Functions
If $f = u + iv$ is analytic, then differentiating the C-R equations:
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(u_x = v_y) \text{ and } \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(u_y = -v_x)$$

Adding: $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$ and $v_{xx} + v_{yy} = 0$

$$\boxed{\nabla^2 u = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \nabla^2 v = 0}$$

Both $u$ and $v$ satisfy **Laplace's equation** — they are harmonic.

**Application:** $u$ can represent velocity potential and $v$ stream function in **irrotational, incompressible flow** (potential flow theory in aerodynamics).

---

### Consequence 2: Orthogonality of Level Curves
The families of curves $u(x,y) = c_1$ and $v(x,y) = c_2$ are **orthogonal** to each other at every intersection point (where $f'(z) \ne 0$).

**In aerodynamics:** Equipotential lines ($u = \text{const}$) and streamlines ($v = \text{const}$) are always perpendicular!

---

### Consequence 3: Conformal Mapping
If $f'(z_0) \ne 0$, the mapping $w = f(z)$ **preserves angles** between curves at $z_0$ (conformal mapping). The angle of rotation = $\arg(f'(z_0))$, scale factor = $|f'(z_0)|$.

---

### Consequence 4: Real and Imaginary Parts Completely Determine Each Other
Given $u$ (harmonic), $v$ (harmonic conjugate) is uniquely determined up to a constant — via C-R equations and integration. You cannot choose $u$ and $v$ independently — they are inextricably linked by C-R.

---

## ⚡ AIR-1 Speed Tricks & Mnemonics

### 🚀 Trick 1: The "Diagonal-Antidiagonal" Memory for C-R

Arrange the four partial derivatives in a 2×2 grid:
$$\begin{array}{c|c}
u_x & u_y \\
\hline
v_x & v_y
\end{array}$$

**C-R says:**
- **Main diagonal:** $u_x = v_y$ → "Top-Left = Bottom-Right" (equal!)
- **Anti-diagonal:** $u_y = -v_x$ → "Top-Right = −Bottom-Left" (opposite signs!)

> This 2×2 table is the fastest way to remember C-R without confusion.

---

### 🚀 Trick 2: The "SAME-OPPOSITE" Rule
$$u_x \underbrace{=}_{\text{SAME}} v_y \qquad u_y \underbrace{=}_{\text{OPPOSITE}}-v_x$$

- $x$ derivative of $u$ **=** $y$ derivative of $v$ (SAME positions, SAME sign)
- $y$ derivative of $u$ **=** MINUS $x$ derivative of $v$ (crossed positions, OPPOSITE sign)

---

### 🚀 Trick 3: Test $|z|^2$ — Differentiate at Origin
$f(z) = |z|^2 = x^2+y^2+0i$: $u = x^2+y^2$, $v = 0$.
- $u_x = 2x$, $v_y = 0$: equal only when $x=0$
- $u_y = 2y$, $-v_x = 0$: equal only when $y=0$
- C-R holds only at origin $(0,0)$ → differentiable at $z=0$ but NOT analytic anywhere

GATE frequently tests this: **$|z|^2$ is differentiable exactly once (at $z=0$) but never analytic.**

---

### 🚀 Trick 4: Compute $f'(z)$ at Specific Points Without Full C-R
Once you've verified analyticity, compute $f'(z)$ using $f'(z) = u_x + iv_x$ and substitute $x, y$ from $z = x + iy$:
- If $f(z) = z^n$ then $f'(z) = nz^{n-1}$ (works exactly like real calculus!)
- If $f(z) = e^z$ then $f'(z) = e^z$
- If $f(z) = \sin z$ then $f'(z) = \cos z$

> ⚡ All the real differentiation rules (product, chain, etc.) work identically for analytic functions!

---

### 🧠 Mnemonic: "**UV Cross Match**"

$$\underbrace{u_x = v_y}_{\text{Across: x↔y, same sign}} \qquad \underbrace{u_y = -v_x}_{\text{Across: y↔x, flip sign}}$$

- When you **cross** (x matches with y, y with x) on the SAME function ($u$ or $v$): **same sign**
- When you **cross** between DIFFERENT functions ($u_y$ vs $v_x$): **flip sign**

---

### 🧠 Mnemonic: "**CURE**" for C-R Verification Steps
1. **C**ompute $u$ and $v$ by separating real and imaginary parts
2. **U**nit-differentiate: find $u_x, u_y, v_x, v_y$
3. **R**ule check: verify $u_x = v_y$ AND $u_y = -v_x$
4. **E**valuate $f'(z)$ using $f'(z) = u_x + iv_x$ if analytic

## 📝 Step-by-Step Solved GATE Examples

### Example 1: Full C-R Check for a Polynomial (GATE 1-Marker)
**Problem:** Is $f(z) = z^2 + 2iz$ analytic? If yes, find $f'(z)$.

**Solution:**
$f(z) = (x+iy)^2 + 2i(x+iy) = x^2-y^2+2ixy + 2ix - 2y$
$= (x^2-y^2-2y) + i(2xy+2x)$

So $u = x^2 - y^2 - 2y$, $v = 2xy + 2x$.

Check C-R:
- $u_x = 2x$, $v_y = 2x$ → **Equal** ✅
- $u_y = -2y-2$, $-v_x = -(2y+2) = -2y-2$ → **Equal** ✅

C-R holds everywhere (partials are continuous polynomials) → **Analytic everywhere (entire).**

$$f'(z) = u_x + iv_x = 2x + i(2y+2) = 2(x+iy) + 2i = 2z + 2i$$

> **Shortcut check:** $\frac{d}{dz}(z^2+2iz) = 2z + 2i$ ✅ (real-calculus rules work!)

$$\boxed{f'(z) = 2z + 2i, \quad f \text{ is entire}}$$

---

### Example 2: C-R Failure Mode (GATE MCQ Trap)
**Problem:** Consider the function $f(z) = |z|^2$. Which statement is TRUE?
(a) Analytic everywhere  
(b) Analytic nowhere  
(c) Differentiable at $z = 0$ only but not analytic anywhere  
(d) Analytic for $|z| > 0$

**Solution:**
$f(z) = x^2+y^2 + 0 \cdot i$: $u = x^2+y^2$, $v = 0$.

- $u_x = 2x$, $v_y = 0$: equal iff $x = 0$
- $u_y = 2y$, $-v_x = 0$: equal iff $y = 0$

C-R holds only at $z = 0$. So $f$ is differentiable at $z=0$ only (but not in any neighborhood of $z=0$).

$$\boxed{\text{Answer: (c) Differentiable at } z=0 \text{ only, but NOT analytic anywhere}}$$

$f'(0) = u_x(0,0) + iv_x(0,0) = 0 + 0 = 0$

---

### Example 3: Finding $b$ for Analyticity (GATE 2-Marker)
**Problem:** For what value of $b$ is $f(z) = x^2 - by^2 + 2ixy$ analytic?

**Solution:**
$u = x^2 - by^2$, $v = 2xy$.

C-R equation 1: $u_x = v_y$
$$2x = 2x \quad \Rightarrow \text{always satisfied}$$

C-R equation 2: $u_y = -v_x$
$$-2by = -2y \quad \Rightarrow b = 1$$

$$\boxed{b = 1}$$

> **Sanity check:** With $b=1$: $u = x^2 - y^2$, $v = 2xy$, so $f(z) = x^2-y^2+2ixy = (x+iy)^2 = z^2$ ✅

---

### Example 4: Polar C-R Check (GATE 3-Marker)
**Problem:** Show that $f(z) = \sqrt{r}\,e^{i\theta/2}$ is analytic for $r > 0$, $-\pi < \theta < \pi$.

**Solution:**
$f(z) = \sqrt{r}\cos(\theta/2) + i\sqrt{r}\sin(\theta/2)$

So $u = \sqrt{r}\cos(\theta/2)$, $v = \sqrt{r}\sin(\theta/2)$.

**Polar C-R equations:**

$u_r = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\cos(\theta/2)$

$\frac{1}{r}v_\theta = \frac{1}{r}\cdot\sqrt{r}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cos(\theta/2) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\cos(\theta/2)$

→ $u_r = \frac{1}{r}v_\theta$ ✅

$v_r = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\sin(\theta/2)$

$-\frac{1}{r}u_\theta = -\frac{1}{r}\cdot\sqrt{r}\cdot(-\frac{1}{2})\sin(\theta/2) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\sin(\theta/2)$

→ $v_r = -\frac{1}{r}u_\theta$ ✅

Both polar C-R equations satisfied for $r > 0$ → **$f$ is analytic for $r > 0$, $-\pi < \theta < \pi$.**

$$\boxed{f(z) = \sqrt{z} \text{ (principal branch) is analytic for } r>0, -\pi<\theta<\pi}$$

---

### Example 5: Verify Harmonic and Find Conjugate (GATE 4-Marker)
**Problem:** Show $u = 2x(1-y)$ is harmonic and find harmonic conjugate $v$.

**Solution:**
$u_x = 2(1-y)$, $u_{xx} = 0$
$u_y = -2x$, $u_{yy} = 0$
$\nabla^2 u = 0 + 0 = 0$ ✅ **Harmonic.**

**Find $v$:** From C-R: $v_y = u_x = 2(1-y)$
→ Integrate w.r.t. $y$: $v = 2y - y^2 + g(x)$

From C-R: $v_x = -u_y = 2x$
→ $g'(x) = 2x \Rightarrow g(x) = x^2 + C$

$$v = 2y - y^2 + x^2 + C$$

**Express $f(z)$:**
$f(z) = u + iv = 2x(1-y) + i(2y-y^2+x^2+C)$

Milne-Thomson check: put $x=z, y=0$:
$u_x(z,0) = 2$, $u_y(z,0) = -2z$
$f(z) = \int(2 - i(-2z))\,dz = \int(2+2iz)\,dz = 2z + iz^2 + C$

$$\boxed{f(z) = iz^2 + 2z + iC}$$

## ⚠️ Common Exam Traps & Pitfalls

> 🔴 **Trap 1: Confusing Necessary vs. Sufficient**  
> C-R equations are **necessary** for differentiability. They are **sufficient** ONLY when combined with continuity of partial derivatives. GATE may ask "which is correct?" — the answer is "C-R is necessary; C-R + continuous partials → sufficient."

> 🔴 **Trap 2: $u_y = v_x$ (Sign Error!)**  
> The most frequent arithmetic error: writing $u_y = v_x$ (without the minus sign) instead of $u_y = -v_x$. C-R requires a **minus sign** on the off-diagonal equation. Losing this sign → completely wrong answer.

> 🔴 **Trap 3: Forgetting the $\frac{1}{r}$ Factor in Polar C-R**  
> In polar form, the C-R equations are $u_r = \frac{1}{r}v_\theta$ (NOT $u_r = v_\theta$). The factor $r$ in the denominator is essential. Forgetting it fails the verification even for analytic functions.

> 🔴 **Trap 4: "C-R holds at one point → Analytic at that point"**  
> FALSE! C-R holding at an isolated point gives differentiability at that point (with continuous partials) but NOT analyticity. Analyticity requires C-R to hold in a **neighborhood** of the point.
