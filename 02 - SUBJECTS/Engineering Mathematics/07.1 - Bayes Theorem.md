> 🎯 **GATE Weightage:** 1–2 marks per paper (Special Topic). Bayes' theorem is the engine of probabilistic reasoning — it answers the question: *"Given that an event has occurred, what is the probability it came from a particular cause?"* In GATE, this appears as diagnostic test problems (defective parts, faulty machines), signal detection, and conditional probability reasoning.

---

## 📌 Part I: Foundation — Basic Probability (Zero to Topper)

### Sample Space & Events
- **Experiment:** Any process with uncertain outcomes
- **Sample Space $S$:** Set of ALL possible outcomes
- **Event $A$:** Any subset of $S$
- **Complement $A^c$ or $\bar{A}$:** All outcomes NOT in $A$

### Axioms of Probability (Kolmogorov)
For any event $A$:
$$\text{(K1) } P(A) \ge 0 \qquad \text{(K2) } P(S) = 1 \qquad \text{(K3) } P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \text{ if } A \cap B = \emptyset$$

### Key Formulas from Axioms

$$\boxed{P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)}$$

$$\boxed{P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)} \quad \text{(Inclusion-Exclusion)}$$

$$\boxed{P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)} \quad \text{ONLY if } A, B \text{ are independent}$$

---

## 🧠 Part II: Conditional Probability — The Gateway to Bayes

### Definition

The **conditional probability** of event $A$ given that event $B$ has occurred:

$$\boxed{P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad P(B) > 0}$$

**Interpretation:** Out of all trials where $B$ occurred, what fraction also had $A$?

### Multiplication Rule (Rearranging Conditional Probability)

$$\boxed{P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)}$$

### Independent Events
Events $A$ and $B$ are **independent** if:
$$\boxed{P(A \mid B) = P(A) \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)}$$

> ⚡ **Key Distinction:**
> - **Mutually exclusive:** $A \cap B = \emptyset$ → $P(A \cap B) = 0$ (cannot both occur)
> - **Independent:** $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$ (occurrence of one doesn't affect the other)
> - **Mutually exclusive events with $P > 0$ are NEVER independent!**

---

## 🧠 Part III: Law of Total Probability (Foundation for Bayes)

### Partition of Sample Space
Events $B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n$ form a **partition** of $S$ if:
1. They are **mutually exclusive:** $B_i \cap B_j = \emptyset$ for $i \ne j$
2. They are **exhaustive:** $B_1 \cup B_2 \cup \cdots \cup B_n = S$
3. $P(B_i) > 0$ for all $i$

### Law of Total Probability

If $\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n\}$ is a partition of $S$, and $A$ is any event:

$$\boxed{P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i)}$$

**For 2 events (most common in GATE):**
$$P(A) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) + P(A \mid B^c) \cdot P(B^c)$$

> ⚡ **Intuition:** $P(A)$ = weighted average of $P(A|B_i)$ where weights = $P(B_i)$. You're summing up all the "paths" through which $A$ can happen.

---

## 🧠 Part IV: Bayes' Theorem — The Core (MASTER THIS!)

### Derivation (From First Principles — 2 Steps)

**Step 1:** From conditional probability definition:
$$P(B_k \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B_k)}{P(A)} = \frac{P(A \mid B_k) \cdot P(B_k)}{P(A)}$$

**Step 2:** Substitute Law of Total Probability for $P(A)$:

$$\boxed{P(B_k \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_k) \cdot P(B_k)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i)}}$$

### For Two Events (2-Way Bayes — Most Common in GATE)

$$\boxed{P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) + P(A \mid B^c) \cdot P(B^c)}}$$

---

## 🧠 Part V: Terminology — Prior, Likelihood, Posterior

| Term | Symbol | Meaning in Bayes |
|---|---|---|
| **Prior probability** | $P(B_k)$ | Probability of cause $B_k$ BEFORE observing evidence $A$ |
| **Likelihood** | $P(A \mid B_k)$ | Probability of observing evidence $A$ given cause $B_k$ |
| **Posterior probability** | $P(B_k \mid A)$ | Probability of cause $B_k$ AFTER observing evidence $A$ |
| **Marginal/Total probability** | $P(A)$ | Denominator — normalizes the result to $[0,1]$ |

### The Bayes Formula in Words:

$$\text{Posterior} \propto \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior}$$

$$P(\text{cause} \mid \text{effect}) = \frac{P(\text{effect} \mid \text{cause}) \times P(\text{cause})}{P(\text{effect})}$$

---

## 🧠 Part VI: The Systematic GATE Solution Method

For ANY Bayes' theorem problem, use this 4-step framework:

**Step 1 — IDENTIFY the partition $\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n\}$**
(These are the "causes" — machines, factories, hypotheses, etc.)

**Step 2 — LIST all priors $P(B_i)$**
(Given directly, or must sum to 1)

**Step 3 — LIST all likelihoods $P(A \mid B_i)$**
(Probability of the observed "effect" given each "cause")

**Step 4 — COMPUTE using Bayes table:**

| Cause $B_i$ | Prior $P(B_i)$ | Likelihood $P(A \mid B_i)$ | Joint $P(A \cap B_i) = P(A\mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i)$ | Posterior $= \frac{P(A \cap B_i)}{P(A)}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $B_1$ | | | | |
| $B_2$ | | | | |
| $\vdots$ | | | | |
| **Total** | **1** | — | **$P(A)$** | **1** |

> ⚡ **The "Bayes Table" method** eliminates the need to memorize the formula — the denominator $P(A)$ is just the sum of the Joint column!

## ⚡ AIR-1 Speed Tricks & Mnemonics

### 🚀 Trick 1: The "Flip the Fraction" Trick

Bayes' theorem is fundamentally:
$$P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{P(A)}$$

Think of it as: **"Flip the conditioning, scale by ratio of probabilities"**
$$P(B \mid A) = P(A \mid B) \cdot \frac{P(B)}{P(A)}$$

If you know $P(A \mid B)$ and need $P(B \mid A)$: multiply by $P(B)/P(A)$.

---

### 🚀 Trick 2: The Odds Form (Fastest for 2-Hypothesis Problems)

For two competing hypotheses $B$ and $B^c$:

$$\frac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B^c \mid A)} = \frac{P(A \mid B)}{P(A \mid B^c)} \cdot \frac{P(B)}{P(B^c)}$$

$$\underbrace{\text{Posterior Odds}}_{\text{what you want}} = \underbrace{\text{Likelihood Ratio}}_{\text{Bayes Factor}} \times \underbrace{\text{Prior Odds}}_{\text{given}}$$

This form completely **avoids computing the denominator** $P(A)$!

---

### 🚀 Trick 3: Units Check on Posteriors (Sum = 1)
After computing all posterior probabilities $P(B_k \mid A)$, they MUST sum to 1:
$$\sum_{k=1}^n P(B_k \mid A) = 1$$

This is a free verification step. If your posteriors don't sum to 1, you made an error in the Joint column.

---

### 🚀 Trick 4: Read the Question Carefully — Don't Swap A and B!
GATE problems say things like: "A defective item is picked. What is the probability it came from Machine 2?"

- **Given (evidence):** defective item selected → $A$ = "item is defective"
- **Sought (cause):** Machine 2 produced it → $B_2$
- **Answer:** $P(B_2 \mid A)$ ← posterior

Students frequently compute $P(A \mid B_2)$ (the likelihood, which is often given directly!) and report it as the answer. This is the #1 error!

---

### 🧠 Mnemonic: "**PLACE**" for Bayes' Theorem Steps
- **P**artition: identify mutually exclusive, exhaustive causes $B_1, \ldots, B_n$
- **L**ist priors: $P(B_i)$ — sum must be 1
- **A**ssign likelihoods: $P(A \mid B_i)$ — probability of evidence given each cause
- **C**ompute joint: $P(A \cap B_i) = P(A \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i)$
- **E**valuate posterior: $P(B_k \mid A) = P(A \cap B_k) / \sum P(A \cap B_i)$

---

### 🧠 Mnemonic: "**CLAP**" for Bayes' Formula
$$P(B \mid A) = \frac{\mathbf{C}\text{ause-}\mathbf{L}\text{ikelihood} \times \mathbf{A}\text{priori}}{\mathbf{P}\text{(evidence)}}$$

- **C**ause-**L**ikelihood = $P(A \mid B)$
- **A**priori = $P(B)$ (prior)
- **P**(evidence) = $P(A)$ (total probability)

## 📝 Step-by-Step Solved GATE Examples

### Example 1: Classic Factory Problem (GATE 2-Marker, Most Common Type)
**Problem:** A factory has three machines M1, M2, M3 producing bolts. Machine M1 produces 20% of output, M2 produces 30%, and M3 produces 50%. Defect rates are 2%, 3%, and 1% respectively. A bolt is randomly selected and found defective. Find the probability it was produced by M2.

**Solution (using Bayes table):**

**Step 1:** Partition = {M1, M2, M3}

| Cause | Prior $P(M_i)$ | Likelihood $P(D \mid M_i)$ | Joint $P(D \cap M_i)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | 0.20 | 0.02 | $0.20 \times 0.02 = 0.0040$ |
| M2 | 0.30 | 0.03 | $0.30 \times 0.03 = 0.0090$ |
| M3 | 0.50 | 0.01 | $0.50 \times 0.01 = 0.0050$ |
| **Total** | **1.00** | — | $P(D) = \mathbf{0.0180}$ |

**Step 2:** Posterior for M2:
$$P(M2 \mid D) = \frac{0.0090}{0.0180} = \mathbf{0.5} = 50\%$$

> **Verification:** $P(M1\mid D) = 0.0040/0.0180 = 2/9 \approx 22.2\%$, $P(M3\mid D) = 0.0050/0.0180 = 5/18 \approx 27.8\%$. Sum = $22.2 + 50 + 27.8 = 100\%$ ✅

$$\boxed{P(M2 \mid D) = 50\%}$$

---

### Example 2: Medical Diagnostic Test (GATE MCQ Type)
**Problem:** Disease $D$ affects 1% of a population. A test has:
- Sensitivity (true positive rate): $P(\text{test}^+ \mid D) = 0.95$
- Specificity (true negative rate): $P(\text{test}^- \mid \bar{D}) = 0.90 \implies P(\text{test}^+ \mid \bar{D}) = 0.10$

A randomly selected person tests positive. What is $P(D \mid \text{test}^+)$?

**Solution (Bayes table):**

| Hypothesis | Prior | Likelihood $P(T^+ \mid \cdot)$ | Joint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Has disease $D$ | $0.01$ | $0.95$ | $0.01 \times 0.95 = 0.0095$ |
| No disease $\bar{D}$ | $0.99$ | $0.10$ | $0.99 \times 0.10 = 0.0990$ |
| **Total** | **1.00** | — | $P(T^+) = 0.1085$ |

$$P(D \mid T^+) = \frac{0.0095}{0.1085} \approx \mathbf{0.0876} \approx \mathbf{8.76\%}$$

> ⚡ **Counter-Intuitive Result:** Even with a 95% sensitive test, a positive result only means ~9% chance of actually having the disease when prevalence is low (1%). This is the **Base Rate Fallacy** — ignoring the prior! GATE LOVES this concept.

$$\boxed{P(D \mid T^+) \approx 8.76\%}$$

---

### Example 3: Two-Machine Problem (GATE 1-Marker)
**Problem:** Machine A produces 60% of parts; Machine B produces the rest. Machine A has 5% defect rate, Machine B has 10% defect rate. A defective part is found. Find probability it came from Machine A.

**Solution:**
$P(A) = 0.6$, $P(B) = 0.4$, $P(D \mid A) = 0.05$, $P(D \mid B) = 0.10$.

$$P(D) = 0.6(0.05) + 0.4(0.10) = 0.030 + 0.040 = 0.070$$

$$P(A \mid D) = \frac{0.6 \times 0.05}{0.070} = \frac{0.030}{0.070} = \frac{3}{7} \approx \mathbf{42.9\%}$$

$$\boxed{P(A \mid D) = \frac{3}{7} \approx 42.86\%}$$

---

### Example 4: Conditional Probability Without Bayes (GATE 1-Marker)
**Problem:** $P(A) = 0.4$, $P(B) = 0.3$, $P(A \cup B) = 0.6$. Find $P(A \mid B)$.

**Solution:**
$P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) = 0.4 + 0.3 - 0.6 = 0.1$

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{0.1}{0.3} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{3}}$$

$$\boxed{P(A \mid B) = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.333}$$

---

### Example 5: Repeated Application (GATE 3-Marker, Higher Level)
**Problem:** Bag I contains 3 red, 4 blue. Bag II contains 5 red, 6 blue. A bag is selected at random, then a ball is drawn and found red. Find probability it came from Bag I.

**Solution:**
$P(\text{Bag I}) = P(\text{Bag II}) = 0.5$ (random selection)

$P(R \mid \text{Bag I}) = \frac{3}{7}$, $P(R \mid \text{Bag II}) = \frac{5}{11}$

$$P(R) = 0.5 \cdot \frac{3}{7} + 0.5 \cdot \frac{5}{11} = \frac{3}{14} + \frac{5}{22} = \frac{33}{154} + \frac{35}{154} = \frac{68}{154} = \frac{34}{77}$$

$$P(\text{Bag I} \mid R) = \frac{0.5 \cdot \frac{3}{7}}{\frac{34}{77}} = \frac{\frac{3}{14}}{\frac{34}{77}} = \frac{3}{14} \cdot \frac{77}{34} = \frac{3 \times 77}{14 \times 34} = \frac{231}{476} = \frac{33}{68}$$

$$\boxed{P(\text{Bag I} \mid R) = \frac{33}{68} \approx 48.5\%}$$

## 📐 Master Summary: Bayes' Theorem Formulas

| Formula | Use Case |
|---|---|
| $P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$ | Basic conditional probability |
| $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) P(B) = P(B \mid A) P(A)$ | Multiplication rule |
| $P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)$ | Total probability (denominator of Bayes) |
| $P(B_k \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_k) P(B_k)}{\sum_i P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)}$ | Bayes' theorem (full form) |
| $\frac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B^c \mid A)} = \frac{P(A \mid B)}{P(A \mid B^c)} \cdot \frac{P(B)}{P(B^c)}$ | Odds form (skip denominator) |

## ⚠️ Common Exam Traps & Pitfalls

> 🔴 **Trap 1: Confusing $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(B \mid A)$**  
> If "a defective part is found, what's the probability it's from M2?" → you need $P(M2 \mid \text{defective})$, NOT $P(\text{defective} \mid M2)$! The latter is the defect rate (already given). Swap them and you get the wrong number. Always read the question direction carefully: "given A, find probability of B" → $P(B \mid A)$.

> 🔴 **Trap 2: Priors Don't Sum to 1**  
> If "M1 makes 20%, M2 makes 30%..." and only two machines are stated, check if there's a third hidden one. $P(M1) + P(M2) + P(M3) = 1$ must hold. Missing a machine in the partition makes the denominator wrong.

> 🔴 **Trap 3: Mutually Exclusive ≠ Independent**  
> For events with non-zero probability: Mutually exclusive ($P(A \cap B) = 0$) means if one occurs the other cannot. Independent ($P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$) means they don't influence each other. They CANNOT both be true simultaneously (unless one has probability 0).

> 🔴 **Trap 4: Ignoring the Base Rate (The Medical Test Fallacy)**  
> A test with 95% accuracy doesn't mean a positive result means 95% chance of disease — the base rate (prior probability of disease) matters enormously. The lower the prevalence, the more likely a positive test is a false positive. Always include the prior in Bayes' calculation!
