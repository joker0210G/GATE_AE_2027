## 📌 Part I: Definition & Fundamentals of Gradient

### 1. The Vector Differential Operator Del ($\nabla$)

In 3D Cartesian coordinates $(x, y, z)$:

$$\nabla = \hat{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}$$

---

### 2. Definition of Gradient

For a scalar field $\phi(x, y, z)$ (a function returning a single number at each 3D point):

$$\boxed{\nabla\phi = \text{grad}\,\phi = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\hat{i} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\hat{j} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\hat{k}}$$

> 💡 **Key Takeaway:** Gradient takes a **SCALAR field** $\phi$ as input and produces a **VECTOR field** $\nabla\phi$ as output!

---

### 3. Geometric Meaning of Gradient (The 3 Golden Theorems)

#### Theorem 1: Direction of Steepest Ascent
$\nabla\phi$ at a point $P(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ points **strictly in the direction of the MAXIMUM rate of increase** of $\phi$.

#### Theorem 2: Maximum Rate of Change
The **magnitude of the gradient**, $\|\nabla\phi\|$, is equal to the **maximum rate of change** of $\phi$ at that point:

$$\text{Max Rate of Increase of } \phi = \|\nabla\phi\| = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\right)^2}$$

#### Theorem 3: Normal to Level Surfaces
$\nabla\phi$ at any point $P$ on a level surface $\phi(x, y, z) = c$ is **ALWAYS PERPENDICULAR (NORMAL)** to the surface at $P$.

---

## 🧠 Part II: Surface Applications (Normal Vector & Tangent Plane)

### 1. Unit Normal Vector to Surface $\phi(x, y, z) = c$

$$\boxed{\hat{n} = \frac{\nabla\phi}{\|\nabla\phi\|}}$$

---

### 2. Tangent Plane & Normal Line Equations at $P(x_0, y_0, z_0)$

Let $\left.\nabla\phi\right|_P = A\hat{i} + B\hat{j} + C\hat{k}$ (where $A = \phi_x$, $B = \phi_y$, $C = \phi_z$ at point $P$).

#### 📐 Tangent Plane Equation:
$$\boxed{A(x - x_0) + B(y - y_0) + C(z - z_0) = 0}$$

#### 📐 Normal Line Equation (Symmetric Form):
$$\boxed{\frac{x - x_0}{A} = \frac{y - y_0}{B} = \frac{z - z_0}{C}}$$

---

### 3. Angle of Intersection Between Two Surfaces

The angle $\theta$ between two intersecting level surfaces $\phi_1(x, y, z) = c_1$ and $\phi_2(x, y, z) = c_2$ at point $P$ is the angle between their normal vectors $\nabla\phi_1$ and $\nabla\phi_2$:

$$\boxed{\cos\theta = \frac{\nabla\phi_1 \cdot \nabla\phi_2}{\|\nabla\phi_1\| \|\nabla\phi_2\|}}$$

#### 🔑 Orthogonal Surfaces Condition:
Two surfaces are **orthogonal** (perpendicular) at $P$ if and only if:

$$\mathbf{\nabla\phi_1 \cdot \nabla\phi_2 = 0}$$

## 🧠 Part III: Directional Derivative ($D_{\hat{u}}\phi$)

> 📖 **Deep-Dive Topic Note:** [[02 - SUBJECTS/Engineering Mathematics/02.2.2.1 - Directional Derivatives|Directional Derivatives (Limit Definition, Dot Product Master Formula, 4 Extremal Properties, 5 GATE Question Types, 4 Speed Tricks, 5 Solved Examples)]] `#status/first-read`

### 1. Definition & Formula

The **Directional Derivative** measures the rate of change of a scalar function $\phi(x, y, z)$ as you move in the direction of a **UNIT vector $\hat{u}$**:

$$D_{\hat{u}}\phi = \frac{d\phi}{ds} = \nabla\phi \cdot \hat{u} = \|\nabla\phi\| \|\hat{u}\| \cos\theta = \|\nabla\phi\| \cos\theta$$

$$\boxed{D_{\hat{u}}\phi = \nabla\phi \cdot \hat{u}}$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the gradient vector $\nabla\phi$ and the direction vector $\hat{u}$.

---

### 2. Properties of Directional Derivative

| Direction of $\hat{u}$ | Angle $\theta$ | Directional Derivative $D_{\hat{u}}\phi$ | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same as $\nabla\phi$ | $\theta = 0^\circ$ | $+\|\nabla\phi\|$ | **Maximum rate of increase** |
| Opposite to $\nabla\phi$ | $\theta = 180^\circ$ | $-\|\nabla\phi\|$ | **Maximum rate of decrease** |
| Perpendicular to $\nabla\phi$ | $\theta = 90^\circ$ | $0$ | **Zero change** (along level surface) |

---

### 3. Algorithm: How to Calculate Directional Derivative in GATE

```
Given: Scalar function φ(x,y,z), Point P(x₀,y₀,z₀), Direction vector a = a₁i + a₂j + a₃k

Step 1: Compute partial derivatives φ_x, φ_y, φ_z
Step 2: Evaluate ∇φ at point P → ∇φ|_P
Step 3: Convert direction vector a to UNIT vector û:
        û = a / ||a|| = (a₁i + a₂j + a₃k) / √(a₁² + a₂² + a₃²)
Step 4: Take dot product:
        D_û φ = (∇φ|_P) · û
```

> 🔴 **CRITICAL GATE TRAP:**  
> Students often forget to normalize the direction vector $\vec{a}$ into a **unit vector $\hat{u}$**!  
> If you compute $\nabla\phi \cdot \vec{a}$ instead of $\nabla\phi \cdot \frac{\vec{a}}{\|\vec{a}\|}$, your answer will be scaled by $\|\vec{a}\|$ and wrong!

## 🧠 Part IV: Gradient Properties & Identities for Position Vector $\vec{r}$

Let $\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$ be the position vector, and $r = \|\vec{r}\| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$.

### 🎯 Master Table of Radial Gradient Identities (GATE Favorites!)

| Identity Formula | Expanded Result | Notes / Proof Idea |
|---|---|---|
| $\nabla r$ | $\frac{\vec{r}}{r} = \hat{r}$ | Unit radial vector |
| $\nabla(r^n)$ | $n r^{n-2} \vec{r}$ | General power formula |
| $\nabla\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)$ | $-\frac{\vec{r}}{r^3} = -\frac{\hat{r}}{r^2}$ | Gravitational / Electrostatic field |
| $\nabla(\ln r)$ | $\frac{\vec{r}}{r^2}$ | 2D source/sink potential |
| $\nabla(f(r))$ | $f'(r) \frac{\vec{r}}{r} = f'(r) \hat{r}$ | **Chain rule for radial functions** |
| $\nabla(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{r})$ | $\vec{a}$ | Constant vector $\vec{a}$ |

#### 🔑 General Formula for $f(r)$:
$$\boxed{\nabla(f(r)) = f'(r) \frac{\vec{r}}{r} = f'(r) \hat{r}}$$

---

### Algebraic Properties of Gradient Operator:

1. **Linearity:** $\nabla(c_1 \phi + c_2 \psi) = c_1 \nabla\phi + c_2 \nabla\psi$
2. **Product Rule:** $\nabla(\phi \psi) = \phi \nabla\psi + \psi \nabla\phi$
3. **Quotient Rule:** $\nabla\left(\frac{\phi}{\psi}\right) = \frac{\psi \nabla\phi - \phi \nabla\psi}{\psi^2}$
4. **Chain Rule:** $\nabla(g(\phi)) = g'(\phi) \nabla\phi$

---

## 🧠 Part V: Aerospace & Physics Applications of Gradient

### 1. Aerodynamics — Potential Flow Theory
In irrotational fluid flow, a **Velocity Potential $\phi$** exists such that velocity vector $\vec{V}$ is:

$$\vec{V} = \nabla\phi = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\hat{i} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\hat{j} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\hat{k}$$

- Streamlines are perpendicular to equipotential lines ($\phi = c$).

### 2. Heat Transfer — Fourier's Law of Heat Conduction
Heat flux vector $\vec{q}$ flows in the direction of maximum temperature *decrease*:

$$\vec{q} = -k \nabla T = -k \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\hat{i} + \frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\hat{j} + \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\hat{k}\right)$$

### 3. Dynamics — Conservative Force Fields
A force field $\vec{F}$ is conservative if it is the negative gradient of a scalar potential energy $U$:

$$\vec{F} = -\nabla U$$

## ⚡ AIR-1 Topper Speed Tricks & Mnemonics

### 🚀 Trick 1: Radial Power Gradient Formula — $n r^{n-2} \vec{r}$
Whenever you see $\nabla(r^n)$, **NEVER differentiate $x,y,z$ separately**!
Apply instantly:
$$\nabla(r^n) = n r^{n-2} \vec{r}$$

*Examples:*
- $\nabla(r^3) = 3 r^{3-2} \vec{r} = 3r\vec{r}$ (1 second!)
- $\nabla(r^{-1}) = -1 r^{-3} \vec{r} = -\frac{\vec{r}}{r^3}$ (1 second!)
- $\nabla(r^2) = 2 r^0 \vec{r} = 2\vec{r}$ (1 second!)

---

### 🚀 Trick 2: Directional Derivative Unit Vector Checklist
In GATE questions asking *"Find directional derivative of $\phi$ in direction of $\vec{a}$"*:
1. First check if $|\vec{a}| = 1$.
2. If $|\vec{a}| \neq 1$, divide by $\sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}$ FIRST.
3. Multiplying $\nabla\phi \cdot \vec{a}$ directly is the #1 mistake candidates make!

---

### 🚀 Trick 3: Maximum Rate & Direction without Computation
- Maximum rate of increase of $\phi$ at point $P$ is **$\|\nabla\phi\|$**.
- Direction of maximum increase is **$\frac{\nabla\phi}{\|\nabla\phi\|}$**.
- Maximum rate of decrease is **$-\|\nabla\phi\|$**.
- Direction of zero change is **perpendicular to $\nabla\phi$**.

---

### 🚀 Trick 4: Orthogonal Surfaces Fast Check
Two surfaces $\phi_1 = c_1$ and $\phi_2 = c_2$ are orthogonal at $P$ if:
$$\mathbf{\nabla\phi_1 \cdot \nabla\phi_2 = 0}$$
Dot product of their gradient vectors must be zero!

## 📝 Step-by-Step Solved GATE Examples

### Example 1: Directional Derivative Calculation
**Problem (GATE):** Find the directional derivative of $\phi(x, y, z) = x^2 y z + 4xz^2$ at $P(1, -1, 2)$ in the direction of vector $\vec{a} = 2\hat{i} - \hat{j} - 2\hat{k}$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1:** Calculate $\nabla\phi$:
$$\phi_x = 2xyz + 4z^2 \implies \phi_x(1,-1,2) = 2(1)(-1)(2) + 4(4) = -4 + 16 = 12$$
$$\phi_y = x^2 z \implies \phi_y(1,-1,2) = (1)^2(2) = 2$$
$$\phi_z = x^2 y + 8xz \implies \phi_z(1,-1,2) = (1)^2(-1) + 8(1)(2) = -1 + 16 = 15$$
$$\nabla\phi|_P = 12\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 15\hat{k}$$

**Step 2:** Convert $\vec{a}$ to unit vector $\hat{u}$:
$$\|\vec{a}\| = \sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = \sqrt{9} = 3$$
$$\hat{u} = \frac{2}{3}\hat{i} - \frac{1}{3}\hat{j} - \frac{2}{3}\hat{k}$$

**Step 3:** Compute Directional Derivative:
$$D_{\hat{u}}\phi = \nabla\phi|_P \cdot \hat{u} = (12)\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) + (2)\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right) + (15)\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right)$$
$$= 8 - \frac{2}{3} - 10 = -2 - \frac{2}{3} = \mathbf{-\frac{8}{3}}$$

$$\boxed{D_{\hat{u}}\phi = -\frac{8}{3}}$$

---

### Example 2: Unit Normal Vector & Tangent Plane
**Problem:** Find the unit normal vector and equation of tangent plane to the surface $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9$ at point $P(1, 2, 2)$.

**Solution:**
Let $\phi(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 9 = 0$.

1. Compute $\nabla\phi|_P$:
   $$\nabla\phi = 2x\hat{i} + 2y\hat{j} + 2z\hat{k}$$
   $$\left.\nabla\phi\right|_{(1,2,2)} = 2\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} + 4\hat{k}$$

2. Unit Normal Vector $\hat{n}$:
   $$\|\nabla\phi\| = \sqrt{2^2 + 4^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{4 + 16 + 16} = \sqrt{36} = 6$$
   $$\hat{n} = \frac{2\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} + 4\hat{k}}{6} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{3}\hat{i} + \frac{2}{3}\hat{j} + \frac{2}{3}\hat{k}}$$

3. Tangent Plane Equation:
   $$A(x - x_0) + B(y - y_0) + C(z - z_0) = 0$$
   $$2(x - 1) + 4(y - 2) + 4(z - 2) = 0 \implies x + 2y + 2z - 9 = 0$$

$$\boxed{\text{Unit Normal: } \frac{1}{3}\hat{i} + \frac{2}{3}\hat{j} + \frac{2}{3}\hat{k}, \quad \text{Tangent Plane: } x + 2y + 2z = 9}$$

---

### Example 3: Maximum Rate of Change
**Problem:** For $\phi(x, y, z) = x^3 - y^3 + z^3$, find the maximum rate of increase at $(1, 1, 1)$ and the direction in which it occurs.

**Solution:**
1. $\nabla\phi = 3x^2\hat{i} - 3y^2\hat{j} + 3z^2\hat{k}$
2. Evaluate at $(1,1,1)$:
   $$\left.\nabla\phi\right|_{(1,1,1)} = 3\hat{i} - 3\hat{j} + 3\hat{k}$$
3. Maximum rate of increase $= \|\nabla\phi\| = \sqrt{3^2 + (-3)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{27} = \mathbf{3\sqrt{3}}$
4. Direction of maximum increase $= \frac{\nabla\phi}{\|\nabla\phi\|} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\hat{i} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\hat{j} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\hat{k}}$

$$\boxed{\text{Max Rate} = 3\sqrt{3}, \quad \text{Direction} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(\hat{i} - \hat{j} + \hat{k})}$$

---

### Example 4: Orthogonal Surfaces
**Problem:** Find $a$ and $b$ such that the surface $a x^2 - b y z = (a+2) x$ is orthogonal to the surface $4 x^2 y + z^3 = 4$ at $(1, -1, 2)$.

**Solution:**
Let $\phi_1 = a x^2 - b y z - (a+2)x = 0$  
Let $\phi_2 = 4 x^2 y + z^3 - 4 = 0$

1. Compute $\nabla\phi_1$ at $(1, -1, 2)$:
   $$\nabla\phi_1 = (2ax - a - 2)\hat{i} - (bz)\hat{j} - (by)\hat{k}$$
   $$\left.\nabla\phi_1\right|_{(1,-1,2)} = (a - 2)\hat{i} - 2b\hat{j} + b\hat{k}$$

2. Compute $\nabla\phi_2$ at $(1, -1, 2)$:
   $$\nabla\phi_2 = (8xy)\hat{i} + (4x^2)\hat{j} + (3z^2)\hat{k}$$
   $$\left.\nabla\phi_2\right|_{(1,-1,2)} = -8\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} + 12\hat{k}$$

3. Point $(1, -1, 2)$ must lie on surface $\phi_1$:
   $$a(1)^2 - b(-1)(2) = (a+2)(1) \implies a + 2b = a + 2 \implies 2b = 2 \implies \mathbf{b = 1}$$

4. Orthogonality condition $\nabla\phi_1 \cdot \nabla\phi_2 = 0$:
   $$(a - 2)(-8) + (-2b)(4) + (b)(12) = 0$$
   $$-8a + 16 - 8b + 12b = 0 \implies -8a + 16 + 4b = 0$$
   Substitute $b = 1$:
   $$-8a + 16 + 4 = 0 \implies -8a + 20 = 0 \implies 8a = 20 \implies \mathbf{a = \frac{5}{2}}$$

$$\boxed{a = \frac{5}{2}, \quad b = 1}$$

## ⚠️ Common Exam Traps & Pitfalls

> 🔴 **Trap 1: Not Normalizing the Direction Vector**  
> *Mistake:* Calculating $D_{\vec{a}}\phi = \nabla\phi \cdot \vec{a}$ using a non-unit vector $\vec{a}$.  
> *Correction:* **Always** divide by $\|\vec{a}\|$: $D_{\hat{u}}\phi = \nabla\phi \cdot \frac{\vec{a}}{\|\vec{a}\|}$.

> 🔴 **Trap 2: Confusing Gradient (Vector) with Directional Derivative (Scalar)**  
> *Mistake:* Writing $\nabla\phi$ as a number or $D_{\hat{u}}\phi$ as a vector.  
> *Correction:*  
> - $\nabla\phi$ is a **VECTOR** ($\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\hat{i} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\hat{j} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\hat{k}$).  
> - Directional derivative $D_{\hat{u}}\phi = \nabla\phi \cdot \hat{u}$ is a **SCALAR** (number).

> 🔴 **Trap 3: Sign Error in Maximum Rate of Decrease**  
> *Mistake:* Rate of steepest decrease is $+\|\nabla\phi\|$.  
> *Correction:* Maximum rate of decrease is **$-\|\nabla\phi\|$** in direction **$-\nabla\phi$**.

> 🔴 **Trap 4: Forgetting Chain Rule in Radial Functions**  
> *Mistake:* $\nabla(r^3) = 3r^2$.  
> *Correction:* $\nabla(r^3) = 3r^2 \nabla r = 3r^2 \frac{\vec{r}}{r} = 3r\vec{r}$ (a vector!).
