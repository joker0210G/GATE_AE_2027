## 📌 1. What is This Topic? (Zero to AIR-1 Overview)

**Fluid Kinematics** is the branch of fluid mechanics that describes the **geometry of fluid motion in space and time**, completely independent of the forces and moments causing that motion (which are governed by Fluid Dynamics / Navier-Stokes / Euler equations).

In GATE Aerospace Engineering, Fluid Kinematics is the essential foundation for **Section 3 (Aerodynamics, ~18–22 marks)**. It provides the mathematical language and tools required to:
1. **Describe velocity and acceleration fields:** Transition between Eulerian (spatial field) and Lagrangian (particle tracking) viewpoints via the **Material Derivative** ($D/Dt$).
2. **Visualize flow patterns:** Formulate and distinguish between **Streamlines**, **Pathlines**, **Streaklines**, and **Timelines** (which coincide in steady flow, but diverge sharply in unsteady flows).
3. **Decompose fluid parcel motion (Helmholtz Decomposition):** Break general fluid motion into pure **translation**, **linear strain rate (volumetric dilatation)**, **shear strain rate**, and **rigid-body rotation / vorticity**.
4. **Quantify fluid rotation & vortex strength:** Evaluate **Vorticity** ($\vec{\omega} = \nabla \times \vec{V}$), **Angular Velocity** ($\vec{\Omega} = \frac{1}{2}\vec{\omega}$), and **Circulation** ($\Gamma = \oint \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{r} = \iint \omega_z dA$), which directly determines aerodynamic lift via the Kutta-Joukowski theorem ($L' = \rho_\infty V_\infty \Gamma$).
5. **Formulate 2D flow fields:** Exploit the **Stream Function** ($\psi$) for incompressible flow and the **Velocity Potential** ($\phi$) for irrotational flow, proving their orthogonal geometric coupling via Cauchy-Riemann equations.

> 💡 **GATE Target:** 2–3 direct questions (MCQ/MSQ/NAT, ~3–5 marks) in Aerodynamics every single year, plus vital conceptual prerequisites for Potential Flow Theory, Boundary Layer Theory, and Compressible Aerodynamics.

## 🧠 Part I: Velocity Field, Eulerian vs. Lagrangian Descriptions & The Material Derivative

### 1. Lagrangian vs. Eulerian Descriptions of Fluid Flow

To describe the motion of a continuous fluid medium, two classical frameworks exist:

```
                  FLUID FLOW DESCRIPTIONS
               ┌────────────────────────────┐
               │                            │
      LAGRANGIAN FRAMEWORK          EULERIAN FRAMEWORK
   (Individual Particle Tracking)   (Fixed Spatial Field Window)
   • Tracks fluid particle #i       • Observes fixed point (x,y,z)
   • Position: r_i = r_i(t)         • Velocity: V = V(x,y,z,t)
   • Newton's 2nd law directly      • Field derivatives & Fluxes
```

1. **Lagrangian Description (Particle Tracking):**
   - Focuses on individual, identifiable fluid parcels as they move through space.
   - Position of parcel labeled by initial coordinate $\vec{r}_0$ is tracked as $\vec{r} = \vec{r}(\vec{r}_0, t)$.
   - Velocity is simply $\vec{V} = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt}$.
2. **Eulerian Description (Spatial Field Description — Standard in Aerodynamics):**
   - Focuses on fixed control locations $(x, y, z)$ in space and monitors the flow variables as functions of space and time:
     $$\vec{V}(x,y,z,t) = u(x,y,z,t)\hat{i} + v(x,y,z,t)\hat{j} + w(x,y,z,t)\hat{k}$$
     $$p = p(x,y,z,t), \quad \rho = \rho(x,y,z,t), \quad T = T(x,y,z,t)$$

---

### 2. The Material (Substantial) Derivative Operator

When a fluid particle moves through a spatial Eulerian field, its rate of change of any property $Q(x,y,z,t)$ (scalar or vector) contains two distinct physical contributions:
1. **Local (Temporal) Rate of Change:** Change due to unsteadiness at that fixed location.
2. **Convective (Advective) Rate of Change:** Change due to the particle physically moving across spatial gradients of $Q$.

By applying the multivariable chain rule along the particle's trajectory ($dx = u\,dt, dy = v\,dt, dz = w\,dt$):
$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{dt}$$

This defines the fundamental **Material (Substantial / Total) Derivative Operator**:

$$\frac{D}{Dt} \equiv \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + (\vec{V} \cdot \nabla)$$

---

### 3. Acceleration Field of a Fluid Particle

Applying the material derivative to the velocity vector $\vec{V}$ yields the fluid **acceleration vector field** $\vec{a}$:

$$\vec{a} = \frac{D\vec{V}}{Dt} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial\vec{V}}{\partial t}}_{\text{Local / Unsteady Accel.}} + \underbrace{(\vec{V}\cdot\nabla)\vec{V}}_{\text{Convective / Advective Accel.}}$$

#### A. Cartesian Component Breakdown:
$$\begin{aligned}
a_x &= \frac{Du}{Dt} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} \\
a_y &= \frac{Dv}{Dt} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} \\
a_z &= \frac{Dw}{Dt} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial w}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial w}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}
\end{aligned}$$

#### B. Cylindrical Polar Coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$:
With velocity $\vec{V} = v_r \hat{e}_r + v_\theta \hat{e}_\theta + v_z \hat{e}_z$:
$$\begin{aligned}
a_r &= \frac{\partial v_r}{\partial t} + v_r\frac{\partial v_r}{\partial r} + \frac{v_\theta}{r}\frac{\partial v_r}{\partial \theta} + v_z\frac{\partial v_r}{\partial z} - \frac{v_\theta^2}{r} \quad (\text{Centripetal term: } -v_\theta^2/r) \\
a_\theta &= \frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial t} + v_r\frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial r} + \frac{v_\theta}{r}\frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial \theta} + v_z\frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial z} + \frac{v_r v_\theta}{r} \quad (\text{Coriolis term: } +v_r v_\theta/r) \\
a_z &= \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial t} + v_r\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial r} + \frac{v_\theta}{r}\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial \theta} + v_z\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}
\end{aligned}$$

> ⚠️ **Key Physical Insight for GATE:**
> - In a **steady converging nozzle** ($\partial/\partial t = 0$), the local acceleration is ZERO, but the fluid accelerates strongly because of **convective acceleration** ($u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} > 0$).
> - In an **unsteady uniform flow** ($\vec{V} = V_0(t)\hat{i}$), spatial derivatives are ZERO, so convective acceleration is ZERO, but fluid experiences **local acceleration** ($\frac{dV_0}{dt}$).

## 🧠 Part II: Flow Visualization Lines (Streamlines, Pathlines, Streaklines & Timelines)

Visualizing fluid flow requires distinct line concepts depending on whether we examine instantaneous velocity directions, particle histories, or continuous dye filaments.

```
┌─────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Line Type       │ Fundamental Definition & Governing Principle                                │
├─────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Streamline   │ Instantaneous line everywhere TANGENT to local velocity vector V(x,y,z,t)   │
│ 2. Pathline     │ Actual TRAJECTORY traced out by a single individual fluid particle in time  │
│ 3. Streakline   │ Instantaneous LOCUS of all particles passed through a fixed injection point │
│ 4. Timeline     │ Instantaneous LINE formed by adjacent fluid particles marked at initial t_0 │
└─────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

---

### 1. Streamlines

A **Streamline** is an instantaneous curve in the flow field such that the tangent at every point along the curve is in the direction of the velocity vector $\vec{V}$ at that exact instant.

```
             d r = (dx, dy, dz)
                ▲
               /  (Collinear: dr || V)
              /
             * ─────────────► V = (u, v, w)
          (x, y, z)
```

#### Mathematical Formulation:
Since the displacement vector along the streamline $d\vec{r} = dx\,\hat{i} + dy\,\hat{j} + dz\,\hat{k}$ is collinear with $\vec{V} = u\,\hat{i} + v\,\hat{j} + w\,\hat{k}$:
$$d\vec{r} \times \vec{V} = \vec{0}$$

Expanding the cross product:
$$\begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ dx & dy & dz \\ u & v & w \end{vmatrix} = (w\,dy - v\,dz)\hat{i} - (w\,dx - u\,dz)\hat{j} + (v\,dx - u\,dy)\hat{k} = \vec{0}$$

This yields the **Standard Differential Equations of a Streamline**:

$$\frac{dx}{u} = \frac{dy}{v} = \frac{dz}{w} \quad (\text{at a fixed instant } t = t_0)$$

In 2D Planar Flow:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{v}{u} \iff u\,dy - v\,dx = 0$$

#### Invariant Properties of Streamlines:
1. **Frozen Time ($t = t_0$):** In evaluating a streamline, time $t$ is treated as a **constant parameter** (a photographic snapshot of the flow field).
2. **Non-Crossing Rule:** Streamlines can **never cross each other** in a regular flow field. If two streamlines crossed, the velocity vector at that point would have two distinct directions simultaneously, violating the continuum hypothesis. *(Exceptions occur only at stagnation points where $\vec{V} = \vec{0}$ or singular points).*
3. **Impermeability (No Flow Across):** Since $\vec{V}$ is strictly tangent to the streamline, the normal velocity component is identically zero ($V_n = 0$). **No fluid can cross a streamline**. (A solid wall in inviscid flow acts exactly as a streamline!).

---

### 2. Pathlines

A **Pathline** (or particle trajectory) is the actual geometric path traced out by an individual, identifiable fluid particle over an elapsed time interval as it advects through the flow.

```
       t = t_0              t = t_1                   t = t_2
          ● ───────────────────► ● ──────────────────────► ●
       (x_0, y_0, z_0)       (x_1, y_1, z_1)           (x_2, y_2, z_2)
       [Particle #A] ─────── Actual Physical Trajectory ──────►
```

#### Mathematical Formulation (Lagrangian Initial Value Problem):
For a particle located at $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ at initial time $t = t_0$:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = u(x,y,z,t), \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = v(x,y,z,t), \quad \frac{dz}{dt} = w(x,y,z,t)$$

Subject to Initial Conditions:
$$x(t_0) = x_0, \quad y(t_0) = y_0, \quad z(t_0) = z_0$$

Integrating these ODEs gives the parametric equations of the pathline: $x = x(t; x_0, t_0)$, $y = y(t; y_0, t_0)$, $z = z(t; z_0, t_0)$. Eliminating time $t$ yields the geometric equation of the pathline.

---

### 3. Streaklines

A **Streakline** is the instantaneous locus of all fluid particles that have previously passed through a single, fixed point in space $\vec{x}_0 = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ at past injection times $\tau \le t$.

```
                            Fixed Injection Point (x_0, y_0)
                                       ▼ (Smoke needle / Dye port)
                                       [●]
                                      /   \
                                     /     \
                                    ●       ●
                             (injected at   (injected at
                               τ = t_2)       τ = t_1 < t_2)
                                              \
                                               ● (injected at τ = t_0 < t_1)
   Instantaneous Curve connecting ALL marked particles at current time t = STREAKLINE
```

#### Systematic 3-Step Mathematical Derivation for GATE:
1. **Step 1:** Solve the particle trajectory (pathline) for a particle injected at $(x_0, y_0)$ at injection time $\tau$:
   $$\frac{dx}{dt} = u(x,y,t), \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = v(x,y,t) \implies x = f(t, \tau, x_0), \quad y = g(t, \tau, y_0)$$
   *(with boundary condition $x(\tau) = x_0, y(\tau) = y_0$).*
2. **Step 2:** Keep the observation time $t$ fixed as a parameter.
3. **Step 3:** Eliminate the injection time variable $\tau$ between $x = f(t, \tau)$ and $y = g(t, \tau)$ to obtain the spatial streakline equation $F(x, y, t) = 0$.

---

### 4. The Master Equivalence Theorem: Steady vs. Unsteady Flow

> 🏆 **THE GOLDEN THEOREM OF FLOW VISUALIZATION:**
> 1. **In Steady Flow ($\frac{\partial\vec{V}}{\partial t} = \vec{0}$):**
>    $$\mathbf{Streamlines \equiv Pathlines \equiv Streaklines}$$
>    All three curves are geometrically identical in steady flow!
> 2. **In Unsteady Flow ($\frac{\partial\vec{V}}{\partial t} \neq \vec{0}$):**
>    $$\mathbf{Streamlines \neq Pathlines \neq Streaklines}$$
>    Streamlines (snapshot of instantaneous velocities), Pathlines (time-history of one particle), and Streaklines (snapshot of many particles from one port) generally diverge and form completely different geometric curves!

## 🧠 Part III: Fluid Element Kinematics & Helmholtz Decomposition

A general infinitesimal fluid element moving through a flow field undergoes four fundamental kinematic motions simultaneously (Helmholtz's Velocity Decomposition Theorem):

$$\text{General Fluid Motion} = \underbrace{\text{Translation}}_{\text{Linear Velocity } \vec{V}} + \underbrace{\text{Linear Deformation}}_{\text{Extensional Strain Rates } \dot{\epsilon}_{ii}} + \underbrace{\text{Angular Deformation}}_{\text{Shear Strain Rates } \dot{\gamma}_{ij}} + \underbrace{\text{Rigid Body Rotation}}_{\text{Angular Velocity / Vorticity } \vec{\Omega}, \vec{\omega}}$$

```
                 HELMHOLTZ VELOCITY DECOMPOSITION
               ┌─────────────────────────────────┐
               │  Velocity Gradient Tensor ∇V   │
               └────────────────┬────────────────┘
                                │
               ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
               │                                 │
     Symmetric Part S_ij               Antisymmetric Part Ω_ij
    (Rate of Strain Tensor)             (Rate of Rotation Tensor)
    • Linear strain rates ε_ii          • Angular velocity vector Ω
    • Volumetric dilatation ∇·V        • Vorticity vector ω = 2Ω
    • Shear strain rates γ_ij
```

---

### 1. Pure Translation
The entire fluid parcel moves rigidly with the local center-of-mass velocity:
$$\vec{V} = u\,\hat{i} + v\,\hat{j} + w\,\hat{k}$$

---

### 2. Linear Strain Rate & Volumetric Dilatation Rate

Linear strain rate is the rate of change of length per unit length of an infinitesimal fluid segment aligned along a coordinate axis:
$$\dot{\epsilon}_{xx} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}, \quad \dot{\epsilon}_{yy} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}, \quad \dot{\epsilon}_{zz} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial z}$$

#### Volumetric Dilatation Rate ($\dot{\Delta}$):
The fractional rate of change of volume of an infinitesimal fluid element $\delta \mathcal{V} = \delta x\,\delta y\,\delta z$:
$$\dot{\Delta} \equiv \frac{1}{\delta \mathcal{V}}\frac{D(\delta \mathcal{V})}{Dt} = \dot{\epsilon}_{xx} + \dot{\epsilon}_{yy} + \dot{\epsilon}_{zz} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = \nabla \cdot \vec{V}$$

> 🔑 **Incompressibility Condition:**
> A fluid flow is **incompressible** if and only if the volume of every fluid element remains constant as it moves:
> $$\frac{D\rho}{Dt} = 0 \iff \nabla \cdot \vec{V} = 0 \iff \text{Volumetric Dilatation Rate } \dot{\Delta} = 0$$

---

### 3. Shear Strain Rate (Angular Deformation)

Shear strain rate represents the rate of decrease of the angle between two mutually perpendicular fluid line segments:
$$\begin{aligned}
\dot{\gamma}_{xy} &= \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \\
\dot{\gamma}_{yz} &= \frac{\partial w}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial z} \\
\dot{\gamma}_{zx} &= \frac{\partial u}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial x}
\end{aligned}$$

The complete **Strain Rate Tensor** is symmetric:
$$S_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} & \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right) & \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right) \\ \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right) & \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} & \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\right) \\ \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right) & \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right) & \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} \end{pmatrix}$$

---

### 4. Angular Velocity (Rate of Rotation) & Vorticity

#### A. Angular Velocity Vector ($\vec{\Omega}$):
The angular velocity of a fluid element is defined as the **average angular velocity of two mutually perpendicular line segments** originally passing through the fluid parcel:

$$\vec{\Omega} = \Omega_x \hat{i} + \Omega_y \hat{j} + \Omega_z \hat{k} = \frac{1}{2}(\nabla \times \vec{V})$$

In 2D Planar $(x,y)$ Flow:
$$\Omega_z = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)$$

In 3D Cartesian Coordinates:
$$\Omega_x = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right), \quad \Omega_y = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right), \quad \Omega_z = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)$$

---

## 🧠 Part IV: Vorticity, Circulation & Vortex Flows

### 1. Vorticity ($\vec{\omega}$ or $\vec{\zeta}$)

**Vorticity** is defined as the curl of the velocity vector field. It is a local point property measuring twice the local angular velocity:

$$\vec{\omega} \equiv \nabla \times \vec{V} = 2\vec{\Omega}$$

$$\vec{\omega} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ u & v & w \end{vmatrix} = \left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right)\hat{i} + \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)\hat{j} + \left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)\hat{k}$$

In Cylindrical Coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$:
$$\omega_z = \frac{1}{r}\left[\frac{\partial (r v_\theta)}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial v_r}{\partial \theta}\right]$$

#### Classification of Flow Fields:
- **Irrotational Flow:** $\vec{\omega} = \nabla \times \vec{V} = \vec{0}$ everywhere in the domain.
- **Rotational Flow:** $\vec{\omega} = \nabla \times \vec{V} \neq \vec{0}$ in some region of the domain (e.g., inside boundary layers, wakes, shock waves, and viscous shear layers).

---

### 2. Circulation ($\Gamma$) and Stokes' Theorem

**Circulation** is a macroscopic, global measure of fluid rotation. It is defined as the **counter-clockwise line integral of the velocity vector along a closed contour $C$**:

$$\Gamma \equiv \oint_C \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{r} = \oint_C (u\,dx + v\,dy + w\,dz)$$

#### Linking Circulation to Vorticity via Stokes' Theorem:
Applying Stokes' Curl Theorem over any open surface $S$ bounded by the closed contour $C$:

$$\Gamma = \oint_C \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \vec{V}) \cdot d\vec{A} = \iint_S \vec{\omega} \cdot \hat{n}\,dA = \iint_S \omega_z\,dA$$

```
               Contour C (Line integral ∮ V·dr = Γ)
               ┌───────────────────────┐
              │                         │
              │     Surface S (Area)    │
              │         ω_z dA          │
              │        ▲  ▲  ▲          │
              │        │  │  │          │
              │        Vorticity Flux   │
               └───────────────────────┘
          Circulation Γ = Total Vorticity Flux through S!
```

> 🔑 **Profound Invariant:**
> 1. **Circulation is the total flux of vorticity** passing through the open surface enclosed by contour $C$.
> 2. If a flow is **irrotational** ($\vec{\omega} = \vec{0}$) throughout a simply connected region, the circulation around **any closed curve** in that region is **identically ZERO** ($\Gamma = 0$).
> 3. If contour $C$ encloses a localized vortex with total circulation $\Gamma_0$, then $\oint_C \vec{V}\cdot d\vec{r} = \Gamma_0$, regardless of the size or shape of the enclosing contour!

---

### 3. Master Comparison: Forced Vortex vs. Free Vortex (GATE Classic)

| Parameter | Forced Vortex (Solid Body Rotation) | Free Vortex (Irrotational Potential Vortex) |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical Mechanism** | Fluid rotated by external mechanical torque (e.g. rotating bucket/impeller) | Fluid swirling freely under angular momentum conservation (e.g. bathtub drain, tornado core exterior) |
| **Tangential Velocity $v_\theta(r)$** | $v_\theta = \omega_0 r$ | $v_\theta = \frac{C}{r} = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$ |
| **Radial Velocity $v_r$** | $0$ | $0$ |
| **Vorticity $\omega_z$** | $\omega_z = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d(r \cdot \omega_0 r)}{dr} = \mathbf{2\omega_0 \neq 0}$ | $\omega_z = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d(r \cdot \frac{C}{r})}{dr} = \mathbf{0} \quad (\text{for } r > 0)$ |
| **Flow Classification** | **ROTATIONAL** everywhere | **IRROTATIONAL** everywhere (singular at origin $r=0$) |
| **Circulation $\Gamma(r)$ (radius $r$)** | $\Gamma = \oint v_\theta (r\,d\theta) = 2\pi \omega_0 r^2 \propto r^2$ | $\Gamma = \oint \left(\frac{\Gamma_0}{2\pi r}\right)(r\,d\theta) = \mathbf{\Gamma_0 = \text{constant}}$ |
| **Streamlines** | Concentric circles ($\psi = -\frac{1}{2}\omega_0 r^2$) | Concentric circles ($\psi = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln r$) |
| **Pressure Profile $p(r)$** | $p(r) = p_0 + \frac{1}{2}\rho \omega_0^2 r^2$ (Parabolic) | $p(r) = p_\infty - \frac{\rho \Gamma^2}{8\pi^2 r^2}$ (Hyperbolic depression) |

## 🧠 Part V: The 2D Stream Function ($\psi$)

### 1. Definition and Mathematical Formulation

For any **2D planar, incompressible flow** ($\nabla \cdot \vec{V} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0$), there exists a continuous scalar function $\psi(x,y,t)$, termed the **Stream Function**, defined such that:

$$u = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}, \qquad v = -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}$$

#### Automatic Satisfaction of Incompressible Continuity:
Substituting the definitions into the 2D continuity equation:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x\partial y} - \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y\partial x} \equiv 0$$
*(By Clairaut-Schwarz theorem on equality of mixed partial derivatives).*

---

### 2. Cylindrical Polar Coordinates $(r, \theta)$

$$v_r = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta}, \qquad v_\theta = -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}$$

Check Incompressible Continuity in 2D Polar:
$$\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r v_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial \theta} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial r\partial\theta} - \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial\theta\partial r} \equiv 0$$

---

### 3. Fundamental Properties of the Stream Function

1. **Lines of Constant $\psi$ are Streamlines:**
   The total differential of $\psi(x,y)$ is:
   $$d\psi = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}dy = -v\,dx + u\,dy$$
   Along a line where $\psi = \text{constant}$ ($d\psi = 0$):
   $$-v\,dx + u\,dy = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{v}{u}$$
   This is precisely the differential equation of a **streamline**!
2. **Volumetric Flow Rate (Discharge) Between Streamlines:**
   The volumetric flow rate $Q$ per unit depth (into the page) passing between two streamlines $\psi = \psi_1$ and $\psi = \psi_2$ along ANY continuous connecting path from point 1 to point 2 is simply:
   $$\Delta Q = Q_{1\to 2} = \int_1^2 \vec{V} \cdot \hat{n}\,ds = \psi_2 - \psi_1$$
3. **Relation to Vorticity:**
   Evaluating the $z$-component of vorticity from the stream function:
   $$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\right) = -\left(\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2}\right) = -\nabla^2\psi$$
   $$\nabla^2\psi = -\omega_z$$
   - If the flow is **rotational**, $\psi$ satisfies the **Poisson Equation** ($\nabla^2\psi = -\omega_z$).
   - If the flow is **irrotational** ($\omega_z = 0$), $\psi$ satisfies the **Laplace Equation**:
     $$\nabla^2\psi = \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2} = 0$$

## 🧠 Part VI: The Velocity Potential ($\phi$)

### 1. Definition and Condition for Existence

For any flow field (2D or 3D, compressible or incompressible) that is **Irrotational** ($\vec{\omega} = \nabla \times \vec{V} = \vec{0}$), there exists a continuous scalar function $\phi(x,y,z,t)$, termed the **Velocity Potential**, such that the velocity vector is the gradient of $\phi$:

$$\vec{V} = \nabla\phi$$

#### Cartesian Components:
$$u = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}, \quad v = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}, \quad w = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}$$

#### Cylindrical Polar Components $(r, \theta, z)$:
$$v_r = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}, \quad v_\theta = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}, \quad v_z = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}$$

#### Mathematical Guarantee of Irrotationality:
Since the curl of the gradient of any twice continuously differentiable scalar field is identically zero:
$$\nabla \times \vec{V} = \nabla \times (\nabla\phi) \equiv \vec{0}$$

> ⚠️ **CRITICAL EXISTENCE CRITERION:**
> - Velocity potential $\phi$ **EXISTS IF AND ONLY IF** the flow is **IRROTATIONAL** ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = \vec{0}$).
> - If a flow has non-zero vorticity ($\vec{\omega} \neq \vec{0}$), $\phi$ **DOES NOT EXIST**.

---

### 2. Incompressible Potential Flow (The Laplace Equation)

When an irrotational flow ($\vec{V} = \nabla\phi$) is also **incompressible** ($\nabla \cdot \vec{V} = 0$):

$$\nabla \cdot (\nabla\phi) = \nabla^2\phi = 0$$

$$\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2} = 0$$

> 🔑 **Linearity & Superposition:**
> Because Laplace's equation is **linear**, if $\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots, \phi_n$ are individual solutions (elementary flows like uniform flow, source, sink, doublet, point vortex), then their linear combination:
> $$\phi_{\text{total}} = \sum_{k=1}^n c_k \phi_k$$
> is also an exact solution! This forms the entire bedrock of **2D Potential Flow Theory**.

---

## 🧠 Part VII: Unified Coupling & Orthogonality of $\phi$ and $\psi$

### 1. The Cauchy-Riemann Relations in Fluid Mechanics

For a **2D Incompressible, Irrotational Flow** (Planar Potential Flow), both the Velocity Potential $\phi(x,y)$ and Stream Function $\psi(x,y)$ exist simultaneously:

$$\begin{aligned}
u &= \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y} \\
v &= \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}
\end{aligned}$$

These are precisely the celebrated **Cauchy-Riemann Equations** from Complex Analysis!

#### The Complex Potential $W(z)$:
We can define an analytic complex potential function of the complex variable $z = x + iy$:
$$W(z) = \phi(x,y) + i\psi(x,y)$$
Its complex derivative gives the conjugate complex velocity:
$$\frac{dW}{dz} = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} + i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} = u - iv$$
$$\left|\frac{dW}{dz}\right| = \sqrt{u^2 + v^2} = \|\vec{V}\| = V$$

---

### 2. Proof of Mutual Orthogonality

Let us find the geometric slope of an **Equipotential Line** ($\phi = c_1$) and a **Streamline** ($\psi = c_2$) in the $(x,y)$ plane:

1. **Slope of Equipotential Line ($\phi(x,y) = c_1$):**
   $$d\phi = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}dy = u\,dx + v\,dy = 0 \implies \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{\phi} = -\frac{u}{v}$$
2. **Slope of Streamline ($\psi(x,y) = c_2$):**
   $$d\psi = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}dy = -v\,dx + u\,dy = 0 \implies \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{\psi} = \frac{v}{u}$$
3. **Product of Slopes:**
   $$\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{\phi} \cdot \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{\psi} = \left(-\frac{u}{v}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{v}{u}\right) = \mathbf{-1}$$

```
                Streamline (ψ = const)
                         ▲
                         │   Slope = +v/u
                         │
      ───────────────────┼───────────────────► Equipotential Line (φ = const)
                         │   Slope = -u/v
                         │   Angle = 90° (Orthogonal Net)
                         │
```

> 🏆 **ORTHOGONALITY THEOREM:**
> Equipotential lines ($\phi = \text{const}$) and Streamlines ($\psi = \text{const}$) intersect everywhere at **right angles ($90^\circ$)**, forming an **Orthogonal Flow Net**.  
> *(The only exception is at stagnation points where $u = v = 0$, where the slopes are indeterminate).*

## ⚡ AIR-1 Topper Mnemonics, Shortcuts & Speed Tricks

### 🚀 Shortcut 1: The 5-Second Streamline Differential Slope
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{v}{u}$$
- *To find the streamline passing through $(x_0, y_0)$:* Separate variables and integrate $\int \frac{dx}{u(x,y)} = \int \frac{dy}{v(x,y)}$, then plug in $(x_0, y_0)$ to fix the constant $C$.
- *Tip:* For linear fields $u = a x, v = b y$, the streamline is immediately $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{b y}{a x} \implies \ln y = \frac{b}{a}\ln x + C \implies y = C x^{b/a}$.

---

### 🚀 Shortcut 2: The "Vorticity = 2 × Angular Velocity" Factor of 2
$$\vec{\omega} = 2\vec{\Omega} = \nabla \times \vec{V}$$
- *Never forget the factor of 2:* If GATE asks for **Rate of Rotation / Angular Velocity** ($\Omega_z$), compute $\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y})$.
- If GATE asks for **Vorticity** ($\omega_z$), compute $(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y})$ without the $1/2$.

---

### 🚀 Shortcut 3: Instant 3-Step Streakline Parametric Elimination Method
When given an unsteady velocity field $(u(x,t), v(y,t))$ and injection point $(x_0, y_0)$:
1. Integrate $\frac{dx}{dt} = u$ from injection time $\tau$ to current time $t$ with $x(\tau) = x_0 \implies x = f(t, \tau)$.
2. Integrate $\frac{dy}{dt} = v$ from injection time $\tau$ to current time $t$ with $y(\tau) = y_0 \implies y = g(t, \tau)$.
3. Solve for $\tau$ in terms of $x$ (or $y$) and substitute into the other coordinate equation!

---

### 🚀 Shortcut 4: Rapid Volumetric Flow Rate without Line Integrals
To find the flow rate $Q$ crossing ANY arbitrary line between point $A(x_1, y_1)$ and point $B(x_2, y_2)$:
$$Q_{A\to B} = |\psi(x_2, y_2) - \psi(x_1, y_1)|$$
Never evaluate cumbersome double or line integrals when the stream function $\psi$ is available!

---

### 🚀 Shortcut 5: Solid Body vs Irrotational Vortex 1-Second Check
- If $v_\theta \propto r$ (speed increases with radius) $\implies$ **Solid Body / Forced Vortex $\implies$ Rotational** ($\omega_z = 2\omega_0$).
- If $v_\theta \propto \frac{1}{r}$ (speed drops inversely with radius) $\implies$ **Free Potential Vortex $\implies$ Irrotational** ($\omega_z = 0$).

## 📝 Step-by-Step Solved GATE Examples (Zero to GATE Topper Level)

### Example 1: Streamline Equation & Acceleration in a 2D Flow Field
**Problem:** A 2D steady, incompressible velocity field is given by $\vec{V} = (2x)\hat{i} - (2y)\hat{j}$ (where coordinates are in meters and velocity in $\text{m/s}$).
1. Determine the equation of the streamline passing through the point $(1, 2)$.
2. Compute the acceleration vector and its magnitude at $(1, 2)$.
3. Verify if the flow is irrotational.

> [!faq]- 🔍 View Solution & Detailed Explanation
> **1. Streamline Equation:**
> For a 2D streamline:
> $$\frac{dx}{u} = \frac{dy}{v} \implies \frac{dx}{2x} = \frac{dy}{-2y}$$
> $$\frac{dx}{x} = -\frac{dy}{y}$$
> Integrating both sides:
> $$\ln x = -\ln y + \ln C \implies \ln(xy) = \ln C \implies xy = C$$
> Since the streamline passes through $(x, y) = (1, 2)$:
> $$C = (1)(2) = 2$$
> **Streamline Equation:** $xy = 2$ (A rectangular hyperbola).
> 
> ---
> **2. Acceleration Vector at $(1, 2)$:**
> Since the flow is steady, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = 0, \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = 0$.
> $$a_x = u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = (2x)(2) + (-2y)(0) = 4x$$
> $$a_y = u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = (2x)(0) + (-2y)(-2) = 4y$$
> At $(x, y) = (1, 2)$:
> $$a_x = 4(1) = 4\text{ m/s}^2, \quad a_y = 4(2) = 8\text{ m/s}^2$$
> $$\vec{a} = 4\hat{i} + 8\hat{j}\text{ m/s}^2$$
> Magnitude:
> $$\|\vec{a}\| = \sqrt{4^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{16 + 64} = \sqrt{80} \approx 8.944\text{ m/s}^2$$
> 
> ---
> **3. Irrotationality Check:**
> $$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial (-2y)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial (2x)}{\partial y} = 0 - 0 = 0$$
> Since $\omega_z = 0$, the flow is **IRROTATIONAL**.

### Example 2: Streamline vs Pathline vs Streakline in Unsteady Flow (GATE Multi-Concept)
**Problem:** An unsteady 2D velocity field is given by $u = x$, $v = y(1 + 2t)$. Fluid particles are released from the point $(1, 1)$ continuously.
1. Find the equation of the **Streamline** passing through $(1, 1)$ at $t = 1\text{ s}$.
2. Find the equation of the **Pathline** for a particle released from $(1, 1)$ at $t = 0$.
3. Find the equation of the **Streakline** at $t = 1\text{ s}$ for particles injected at $(1, 1)$ for times $\tau \le 1$.

> [!faq]- 🔍 View Solution & Detailed Explanation
> **1. Streamline at $t = 1\text{ s}$ through $(1, 1)$:**
> At $t = 1$, time is frozen: $u = x, v = y(1 + 2(1)) = 3y$.
> $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{v}{u} = \frac{3y}{x} \implies \frac{dy}{y} = 3\frac{dx}{x}$$
> Integrating: $\ln y = 3\ln x + \ln C \implies y = C x^3$.
> Passing through $(1, 1)$: $1 = C(1)^3 \implies C = 1$.
> **Streamline at $t=1$:** $y = x^3$.
> 
> ---
> **2. Pathline of particle released at $(1, 1)$ at $t = 0$:**
> $$\frac{dx}{dt} = u = x \implies \frac{dx}{x} = dt \implies \ln\left(\frac{x}{x_0}\right) = t \implies x(t) = 1 \cdot e^t$$
> $$\frac{dy}{dt} = v = y(1+2t) \implies \frac{dy}{y} = (1+2t)dt \implies \ln\left(\frac{y}{y_0}\right) = t + t^2 \implies y(t) = 1 \cdot e^{t+t^2}$$
> Since $x = e^t \implies t = \ln x$:
> Substitute $t = \ln x$ into $y(t)$:
> $$y = e^{\ln x + (\ln x)^2} = e^{\ln x} \cdot e^{(\ln x)^2} = x \cdot e^{(\ln x)^2}$$
> **Pathline Equation:** $y = x \cdot e^{(\ln x)^2}$.
> 
> ---
> **3. Streakline at $t = 1\text{ s}$ for particles injected at $(1, 1)$ at injection times $\tau \in [0, 1]$:**
> Integrate pathline from injection time $\tau$ (where $x=1, y=1$) up to current time $t$:
> $$\int_1^x \frac{dx'}{x'} = \int_\tau^t dt' \implies \ln x = t - \tau \implies \tau = t - \ln x$$
> $$\int_1^y \frac{dy'}{y'} = \int_\tau^t (1+2t')dt' \implies \ln y = (t - \tau) + (t^2 - \tau^2) = (t-\tau)[1 + t + \tau]$$
> At observation time $t = 1$:
> From $x$-equation: $\tau = 1 - \ln x$.
> Substitute $\tau = 1 - \ln x$ into $y$-equation:
> $$(t-\tau) = \ln x$$
> $$(1 + t + \tau) = 1 + 1 + (1 - \ln x) = 3 - \ln x$$
> Therefore:
> $$\ln y = (\ln x)(3 - \ln x) = 3\ln x - (\ln x)^2$$
> Taking exponentials on both sides:
> $$y = e^{3\ln x - (\ln x)^2} = \frac{x^3}{e^{(\ln x)^2}}$$
> **Streakline Equation at $t=1$:** $y = x^3 e^{-(\ln x)^2}$.
> 
> **⚠️ Critical Verification:** At $t=1$ and point $(1,1)$:
> - Streamline: $y = x^3$
> - Pathline: $y = x e^{(\ln x)^2}$
> - Streakline: $y = x^3 e^{-(\ln x)^2}$
> *Notice how all 3 lines pass through $(1,1)$ but diverge into three totally different curves due to unsteadiness!*

### Example 3: Vorticity, Angular Velocity & Circulation via Stokes' Theorem
**Problem:** A 2D velocity field is given by $\vec{V} = (2y)\hat{i} + (4x)\hat{j}$ (in $\text{m/s}$).
1. Calculate the vorticity vector $\vec{\omega}$ and the angular velocity $\vec{\Omega}$.
2. Calculate the circulation $\Gamma$ around a circle of radius $R = 2\text{ m}$ centered at the origin:
   - Method A: By direct line integration $\oint \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{r}$.
   - Method B: By Stokes' Theorem surface integration $\iint \omega_z dA$.

> [!faq]- 🔍 View Solution & Detailed Explanation
> **1. Vorticity and Angular Velocity:**
> $$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial (4x)}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial (2y)}{\partial y} = 4 - 2 = +2\text{ rad/s}$$
> $$\vec{\omega} = 2\hat{k}\text{ rad/s}$$
> Angular Velocity:
> $$\vec{\Omega} = \frac{1}{2}\vec{\omega} = +1\hat{k}\text{ rad/s}$$
> 
> ---
> **2. Circulation $\Gamma$ around Circle of Radius $R = 2\text{ m}$:**
> 
> **Method A: Direct Line Integration:**
> Parameterize the circle of radius $R = 2$:
> $x = 2\cos\theta \implies dx = -2\sin\theta\,d\theta$
> $y = 2\sin\theta \implies dy = 2\cos\theta\,d\theta$
> Along the circle:
> $u = 2y = 4\sin\theta, \quad v = 4x = 8\cos\theta$
> $$\begin{aligned}
> \Gamma &= \oint_C (u\,dx + v\,dy) = \int_0^{2\pi} [(4\sin\theta)(-2\sin\theta\,d\theta) + (8\cos\theta)(2\cos\theta\,d\theta)] \\
> &= \int_0^{2\pi} (-8\sin^2\theta + 16\cos^2\theta)d\theta \\
> &= -8\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta + 16\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta \\
> &= -8(\pi) + 16(\pi) = +8\pi\text{ m}^2\text{/s} \approx 25.133\text{ m}^2\text{/s}
> \end{aligned}$$
> 
> **Method B: Stokes' Theorem:**
> $$\Gamma = \iint_S \omega_z\,dA = \iint_S (2)\,dA = 2 \iint_S dA = 2 \cdot (\text{Area of Circle}) = 2 \cdot (\pi R^2)$$
> With $R = 2\text{ m}$:
> $$\Gamma = 2 \cdot \pi(2^2) = 2 \cdot 4\pi = \mathbf{8\pi\text{ m}^2\text{/s}}$$
> 
> *Stokes' theorem evaluated the circulation in 5 seconds flat!*

### Example 4: Stream Function, Velocity Potential & Volumetric Flow Rate
**Problem:** A flow field is represented by the velocity potential $\phi = 3x^2 - 3y^2$.
1. Verify whether this represents a physically possible incompressible flow field.
2. Find the corresponding Stream Function $\psi(x,y)$, given that $\psi(0,0) = 0$.
3. Compute the volumetric flow rate per unit depth passing across the straight line joining $A(1, 0)$ and $B(2, 3)$.

> [!faq]- 🔍 View Solution & Detailed Explanation
> **1. Physical Incompressibility Verification:**
> An irrotational flow field is physically possible for an incompressible fluid if it satisfies Laplace's equation $\nabla^2\phi = 0$:
> $$\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}(3x^2 - 3y^2) = 6$$
> $$\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2}(3x^2 - 3y^2) = -6$$
> $$\nabla^2\phi = 6 + (-6) = 0 \quad \text{✅ Satisfied! Incompressible flow!}$$
> 
> ---
> **2. Finding the Stream Function $\psi(x,y)$:**
> Velocity components:
> $$u = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} = 6x = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}$$
> $$v = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} = -6y = -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} \implies \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} = 6y$$
> Integrate $\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y} = 6x$ with respect to $y$:
> $$\psi(x,y) = 6xy + f(x)$$
> Differentiate with respect to $x$:
> $$\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} = 6y + f'(x) = 6y \implies f'(x) = 0 \implies f(x) = C$$
> Since $\psi(0,0) = 0 \implies C = 0$.
> **Stream Function:** $\psi(x,y) = 6xy$.
> 
> ---
> **3. Volumetric Flow Rate Across Line $AB$:**
> Using the stream function difference shortcut:
> $$\psi(A) = \psi(1, 0) = 6(1)(0) = 0$$
> $$\psi(B) = \psi(2, 3) = 6(2)(3) = 36\text{ m}^2\text{/s}$$
> $$\Delta Q = |\psi(B) - \psi(A)| = |36 - 0| = \mathbf{36\text{ m}^3\text{/s per meter depth}}$$

### Example 5: Acceleration Field in a 2D Unsteady Converging Duct
**Problem:** In a converging duct, the velocity field along the centerline is modeled as $\vec{V} = \left(2t\left(1 + \frac{x}{L}\right)\right)\hat{i}$ (where $L = 2\text{ m}$). At $t = 3\text{ s}$ and $x = 2\text{ m}$, find:
1. The local acceleration component.
2. The convective acceleration component.
3. The total material acceleration.

> [!faq]- 🔍 View Solution & Detailed Explanation
> Velocity field: $u(x,t) = 2t\left(1 + \frac{x}{2}\right) = 2t + tx, \quad v = 0, \quad w = 0$.
> 
> **1. Local Acceleration:**
> $$a_{\text{local}} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = 2 + x$$
> At $x = 2\text{ m}, t = 3\text{ s}$:
> $$a_{\text{local}} = 2 + 2 = \mathbf{4\text{ m/s}^2}$$
> 
> **2. Convective Acceleration:**
> $$a_{\text{convective}} = u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \left[2t\left(1 + \frac{x}{2}\right)\right] \cdot [t]$$
> At $x = 2\text{ m}, t = 3\text{ s}$:
> $$u(2, 3) = 2(3)\left(1 + \frac{2}{2}\right) = 6(2) = 12\text{ m/s}$$
> $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = t = 3\text{ s}^{-1}$$
> $$a_{\text{convective}} = 12 \times 3 = \mathbf{36\text{ m/s}^2}$$
> 
> **3. Total Material Acceleration:**
> $$a_x = a_{\text{local}} + a_{\text{convective}} = 4 + 36 = \mathbf{40\text{ m/s}^2}$$
> $$\vec{a} = 40\hat{i}\text{ m/s}^2$$

## ⚠️ Common Exam Traps & Pitfalls (Where Marks are Lost)

> 🔴 **Trap 1: Stream Function Sign Convention Error**
> - *Mistake:* Writing $v = +\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}$.
> - *Correction:* The exact definition is $u = +\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}$ and $v = -\mathbf{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}}$. Missing this negative sign flips the direction of the vertical velocity and invalidates Cauchy-Riemann orthogonality.

> 🔴 **Trap 2: Assuming Velocity Potential $\phi$ Exists for Any Flow**
> - *Mistake:* Attempting to find $\phi$ for a rotational shear flow (e.g. Couette flow $u = \frac{U}{h}y, v = 0$).
> - *Correction:* $\phi$ exists **ONLY IF** $\nabla \times \vec{V} = \vec{0}$ (Irrotational). For Couette flow, $\omega_z = -U/h \neq 0$, so velocity potential $\phi$ **DOES NOT EXIST**!

> 🔴 **Trap 3: Equating Streamlines, Pathlines, and Streaklines in Unsteady Flow**
> - *Mistake:* Assuming all three visualization lines are identical in time-dependent flows.
> - *Correction:* Streamlines $\equiv$ Pathlines $\equiv$ Streaklines **ONLY in steady flow**. In unsteady flow ($\partial\vec{V}/\partial t \neq 0$), they are three completely distinct curves.

> 🔴 **Trap 4: Missing the Factor of 2 in Vorticity vs. Angular Velocity**
> - *Mistake:* Setting $\omega_z = \Omega_z$.
> - *Correction:* Vorticity is **TWICE** the angular velocity: $\vec{\omega} = 2\vec{\Omega} \iff \Omega_z = \frac{1}{2}\omega_z$.

> 🔴 **Trap 5: Forgetting to Freeze Time for Streamlines**
> - *Mistake:* Treating time $t$ as a variable when integrating $\frac{dx}{u} = \frac{dy}{v}$ for streamlines in unsteady flows.
> - *Correction:* For streamlines, time $t$ is a **frozen constant snapshot** ($t = t_0$). Time is only dynamic during pathline integration ($dx/dt = u$).

> 🔴 **Trap 6: Incompressibility vs. Irrotationality Confusion**
> - *Mistake:* Believing that an incompressible flow ($\nabla \cdot \vec{V} = 0$) must be irrotational, or vice versa.
> - *Correction:* They are completely independent physical conditions! A boundary layer is incompressible ($\nabla \cdot \vec{V} = 0$) but highly rotational ($\nabla \times \vec{V} \neq 0$). A supersonic expansion wave is compressible ($\nabla \cdot \vec{V} \neq 0$) but irrotational ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$).

| **Free Vortex (Potential Vortex)** | $v_\theta = \frac{\Gamma_0}{2\pi r}, \quad \omega_z = 0 \text{ }(r>0), \quad \Gamma = \Gamma_0$ | Irrotational flow profile |
| **Stream Function Definition (2D)** | $u = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}, \quad v = -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}$ | Incompressible 2D flow (continuity automatic) |
| **Stream Function in Polar (2D)** | $v_r = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta}, \quad v_\theta = -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}$ | Polar incompressible flow |
| **Volumetric Discharge Formula** | $\Delta Q_{1\to 2} = \psi_2 - \psi_1$ | Flow rate per unit depth between streamlines |
| **Velocity Potential Definition** | $\vec{V} = \nabla\phi \iff u = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}, v = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}, w = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}$ | Exists $\iff$ Flow is Irrotational ($\nabla\times\vec{V}=\vec{0}$) |
| **Laplace Equation for $\phi$** | $\nabla^2\phi = \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2} = 0$ | Incompressible + Irrotational flow |
| **Cauchy-Riemann Equations** | $\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y} = u, \quad \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} = v$ | 2D Potential Flow coupling |
| **Orthogonality Condition** | $\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_\phi \cdot \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_\psi = \left(-\frac{u}{v}\right)\left(\frac{v}{u}\right) = -1$ | Equipotential lines $\perp$ Streamlines |
