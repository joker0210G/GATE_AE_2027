## 📌 1. What is This Topic? (Zero to AIR-1 Overview)

A **Pathline** (or particle trajectory) is the physical, time-history path traced out by an individual, identifiable fluid parcel as it travels through a flow field over an elapsed time interval $t \in [t_0, t]$.

While Streamlines represent an instantaneous vector field snapshot and Streaklines represent a collection of many particles released from a single port:
- A **Pathline** follows **Newton's Second Law applied directly to one specific fluid parcel** in the Lagrangian frame of reference.
- Integrating pathlines enables aerodynamicists to determine **fluid residence times** in combustors and boundary layers, evaluate **aircraft droplet impingement & icing limits**, and compute **transit times** through supersonic nozzles.
- In experimental testing, long-exposure photography tracking single illuminated tracer particles or **Particle Tracking Velocimetry (PTV)** directly captures pathlines.

> 💡 **GATE Target:** 1–2 direct questions (MCQ/NAT, ~2–3 marks) in Aerodynamics every year testing pathline ODE integration, residence/transit time calculations, and particle tracer dynamics (Stokes number).

## 🧠 Part I: The Lagrangian Formulation of Pathlines

```
                  LAGRANGIAN PARTICLE TRAJECTORY
               ┌─────────────────────────────────┐
               │  dr/dt = V(x(t), y(t), z(t), t) │
               │  r(t₀) = (x₀, y₀, z₀)           │
               └────────────────┬────────────────┘
                                │
         ┌──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┐
         ▼                      ▼                      ▼
   2D CARTESIAN            3D CARTESIAN        CYLINDRICAL POLAR
    dx/dt = u(x,y,t)        dx/dt = u(x,y,z,t)   dr/dt = v_r(r,θ,t)
    dy/dt = v(x,y,t)        dy/dt = v(x,y,z,t)   r dθ/dt = v_θ(r,θ,t)
                            dz/dt = w(x,y,z,t)   dz/dt = v_z(r,θ,t)
```

---

### 1. The Governing Initial Value Problem (IVP)

For a single fluid particle located at $\vec{r}_0 = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ at initial time $t = t_0$, its position $\vec{r}(t) = (x(t), y(t), z(t))$ at any subsequent time $t \ge t_0$ is governed by the first-order non-linear system of ODEs:

$$\frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = \vec{V}(\vec{r}(t), t)$$

#### Component Breakdown:

1. **3D Cartesian Coordinates:**
   $$\frac{dx}{dt} = u(x, y, z, t), \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = v(x, y, z, t), \quad \frac{dz}{dt} = w(x, y, z, t)$$
   Subject to Initial Conditions:
   $$x(t_0) = x_0, \quad y(t_0) = y_0, \quad z(t_0) = z_0$$

2. **2D Cylindrical Polar Coordinates $(r, \theta)$:**
   $$\frac{dr}{dt} = v_r(r, \theta, t), \qquad r\frac{d\theta}{dt} = v_\theta(r, \theta, t) \iff \frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{v_\theta(r, \theta, t)}{r}$$
   Subject to: $r(t_0) = r_0, \quad \theta(t_0) = \theta_0$.

3. **3D Spherical Polar Coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$:**
   $$\frac{dr}{dt} = v_r, \quad \frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{v_\theta}{r}, \quad \frac{d\phi}{dt} = \frac{v_\phi}{r\sin\theta}$$

---

### 2. Analytical Integration & Elimination of Time

To obtain the explicit spatial curve $y = f(x)$ or $F(x, y, z) = 0$ of a pathline:
1. Integrate the parametric ODEs $\frac{dx}{dt} = u$ and $\frac{dy}{dt} = v$ with respect to time $t$ from $t_0$ to $t$:
   $$x(t) = X(t; x_0, y_0, t_0), \quad y(t) = Y(t; x_0, y_0, t_0)$$
2. Invert one coordinate equation to express time $t$ as a function of space (e.g. $t = g(x; x_0, t_0)$).
3. Substitute $t = g(x)$ into the remaining coordinate equation:
   $$y = Y(g(x; x_0, t_0); x_0, y_0, t_0) \implies y = f(x)$$

## 🧠 Part II: Pathline Kinematics — Arc Length, Speed & Transit Time

```
       t = t₀                                                  t = t_final
          ● ─────────────── Pathline Curve s(t) ────────────────► ●
       (x₀, y₀)                                                (x_f, y_f)
          │                                                       │
          └─────────── Transit Time: Δt = ∫ (1/V) ds ─────────────┘
```

---

### 1. Arc Length Along a Pathline

The infinitesimal differential arc length $ds$ traversed by a fluid particle along its pathline in time interval $dt$ is:
$$ds = \|\vec{V}(t)\| dt = \sqrt{u^2 + v^2 + w^2} \, dt$$

The total **Pathline Distance (Arc Length)** traveled by the fluid particle from time $t_1$ to $t_2$ is:

$$s(t_1 \to t_2) = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \|\vec{V}(t)\| dt = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sqrt{u(t)^2 + v(t)^2 + w(t)^2} \, dt$$

In spatial coordinates along a known trajectory $y = f(x)$:
$$s = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2} \, dx$$

---

### 2. Fluid Transit Time and Residence Time

The time $t_{\text{transit}}$ required for a fluid particle to travel along its pathline between two spatial stations $s_1$ and $s_2$ is given by:

$$t_{\text{transit}} = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} \frac{ds}{V(s)}$$

In 1D Flow ($V = u(x)$):
$$t_{\text{transit}} = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{dx}{u(x)}$$

> 🔑 **Aerodynamic Applications of Transit Time:**
> 1. **Nozzle Transit Time:** Time taken for combustion gases to expand through a rocket or gas turbine nozzle: $t = \int_0^L \frac{dx}{u(x)}$.
> 2. **Combustor Residence Time:** Time available for fuel-air droplet vaporization and chemical reaction: $\tau_{\text{res}} = \frac{\mathcal{V}_{\text{combustor}}}{\dot{Q}} \approx \frac{L}{\bar{u}}$.
> 3. **Boundary Layer Convection Time:** Transit time of disturbance packets leading to laminar-turbulent transition ($e^N$ method).

## 🧠 Part III: Pathlines in Canonical Aerodynamic Flow Fields

```
┌─────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Flow Field Type         │ Velocity Field $\vec{V}$          │ Exact Pathline Trajectory $\vec{r}(t)$   │
├─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Uniform Flow         │ $u = U_0, v = 0$                  │ Straight Line: $x(t) = x_0 + U_0(t-t_0)$ │
│                         │                                   │ $y(t) = y_0$                            │
├─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. Forced Vortex        │ $v_r = 0, v_\theta = \omega_0 r$  │ Concentric Circle: $r(t) = r_0$         │
│    (Solid Body)         │                                   │ $\theta(t) = \theta_0 + \omega_0(t-t_0)$│
├─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. Free Potential       │ $v_r = 0, v_\theta = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$│ Concentric Circle: $r(t) = r_0$         │
│    Vortex               │                                   │ $\theta(t) = \theta_0 + \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r_0^2}(t-t_0)$│
├─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 4. Pure 2D Sink Flow    │ $v_r = -\frac{\Lambda}{2\pi r}, v_\theta = 0$│ Radial Inward Line: $\theta(t) = \theta_0$│
│                         │                                   │ $r(t) = \sqrt{r_0^2 - \frac{\Lambda}{\pi}(t-t_0)}$│
├─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 5. Combined Sink-Vortex │ $v_r = -\frac{\Lambda}{2\pi r}$   │ Inward Logarithmic Spiral:              │
│    (Tornado / Drain)    │ $v_\theta = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$│ $\theta(r) = \theta_0 - \frac{\Gamma}{\Lambda}\ln(r/r_0)$│
└─────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

---

### 1. Sink-Vortex Spiral Pathline Derivation

In a combined line sink ($-\Lambda$) and point vortex ($\Gamma$) at the origin:
$$v_r = \frac{dr}{dt} = -\frac{\Lambda}{2\pi r}, \qquad v_\theta = r\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$$

Dividing the two differential equations:
$$\frac{dr}{r\,d\theta} = \frac{-\Lambda / (2\pi r)}{\Gamma / (2\pi r)} = -\frac{\Lambda}{\Gamma} \implies \frac{dr}{r} = -\frac{\Lambda}{\Gamma} d\theta$$

Integrating from $(r_0, \theta_0)$ to $(r, \theta)$:
$$\ln\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right) = -\frac{\Lambda}{\Gamma}(\theta - \theta_0) \implies \mathbf{r(\theta) = r_0 \exp\left[-\frac{\Lambda}{\Gamma}(\theta - \theta_0)\right]}$$

- **Shape:** An **Inward Logarithmic Spiral**.
- **Time to reach origin ($r = 0$):**
  $$\int_{r_0}^0 2r\,dr = -\int_0^{t_{\text{collapse}}} \frac{\Lambda}{\pi} dt \implies -r_0^2 = -\frac{\Lambda}{\pi} t_{\text{collapse}} \implies \mathbf{t_{\text{collapse}} = \frac{\pi r_0^2}{\Lambda}}$$

## 🧠 Part IV: Particle Tracer Dynamics & The Stokes Number

In experimental wind tunnel testing (PTV, PIV) and aerospace multiphase flows (aircraft icing, droplet impingement, engine sand ingestion), we must know **whether a tracer particle actually follows the fluid pathline or deviates due to particle inertia**.

```
                             FLUID STREAMLINE / PATHLINE
                                  ─────────────────\
                                                     \   Fluid Parcel Path
                                                      \──────────────────
               Small Stk ≪ 0.1                         \
               (Follows pathline faithfully)            ▼
                         ● ──────────────────────────────► Tracer Particle Path
               Large Stk ≫ 1.0                             
               (Inertia causes particle to cross pathline!) ────────► Ballistic Droplet
```

---

### 1. The Particle Momentum Equation (Basset-Boussinesq-Oseen)

For a spherical micro-particle of diameter $d_p$ and density $\rho_p$ suspended in a fluid of dynamic viscosity $\mu$:

$$m_p \frac{d\vec{V}_p}{dt} = \vec{F}_{\text{drag}} = 3\pi \mu d_p (\vec{V}_f - \vec{V}_p) \quad (\text{Stokes Drag for } Re_p < 1)$$

$$\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\rho_p d_p^3\right) \frac{d\vec{V}_p}{dt} = 3\pi \mu d_p (\vec{V}_f - \vec{V}_p)$$

$$\frac{d\vec{V}_p}{dt} = \frac{\vec{V}_f - \vec{V}_p}{\tau_p}$$

where $\tau_p$ is the **Particle Momentum Response Time (Relaxation Time)**:

$$\mathbf{\tau_p = \frac{\rho_p d_p^2}{18 \mu}}$$

---

### 2. The Stokes Number ($Stk$)

The **Stokes Number** ($Stk$) is the non-dimensional ratio of the particle relaxation time $\tau_p$ to the characteristic fluid flow timescale $\tau_f = L/U_0$:

$$\mathbf{Stk \equiv \frac{\tau_p}{\tau_f} = \frac{\rho_p d_p^2 U_0}{18 \mu L}}$$

```
┌─────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Stokes Number   │ Physical Particle Behavior & Aerodynamic Consequence                        │
├─────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ $Stk \ll 0.1$   │ **Perfect Flow Tracer:** Particle inertia is negligible. Particle velocity  │
│                 │ perfectly matches fluid velocity ($\vec{V}_p \approx \vec{V}_f$). Particle  │
│                 │ trajectory is **IDENTICAL to the fluid pathline** (Ideal PIV/PTV seeding!). │
├─────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ $Stk \sim 1.0$   │ **Moderate Slip:** Particle partially follows curved streamlines but slips  │
│                 │ outward due to centrifugal forces in vortices.                              │
├─────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ $Stk \gg 1.0$   │ **Ballistic Trajectory:** Particle inertia dominates completely. Particle   │
│                 │ travels in straight lines unaffected by fluid deflection.                   │
│                 │ *(Primary cause of supercooled water droplet impingement on wing leading    │
│                 │ edges, causing severe **In-Flight Aircraft Icing**).*                       │
└─────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## ⚡ AIR-1 Topper Mnemonics, Shortcuts & Speed Tricks

### 🚀 Shortcut 1: The 1D Transit Time Integration Shortcut
For any linear velocity distribution $u(x) = u_1 + \left(\frac{u_2 - u_1}{L}\right)x$ across a nozzle of length $L$:
$$t_{\text{transit}} = \int_0^L \frac{dx}{u(x)} = \frac{L}{u_2 - u_1} \ln\left(\frac{u_2}{u_1}\right)$$
- *Notice:* The transit time is governed by the **Logarithmic Mean Speed**, NOT the arithmetic average!

---

### 🚀 Shortcut 2: The Particle Relaxation Time Scaling
$$\tau_p \propto d_p^2$$
- Halving the tracer particle diameter ($d_p \to d_p/2$) reduces response time $\tau_p$ by a factor of **4** ($4\times$ faster tracking response!).
- In wind tunnel PIV, keeping particle size $d_p \le 1\,\mu\text{m}$ guarantees $Stk < 0.01$, ensuring the recorded pathlines are true fluid pathlines.

---

### 🚀 Shortcut 3: Instant Identification of Pathline vs Streamline in GATE
- Look at the integration variable:
  - If you integrate with respect to **space** ($\int \frac{dx}{u} = \int \frac{dy}{v}$) keeping $t$ fixed $\implies$ **Streamline**.
  - If you integrate with respect to **time** ($\int dx = \int u\,dt, \int dy = \int v\,dt$) $\implies$ **Pathline**.

## 📝 Step-by-Step Solved GATE Examples (Zero to GATE Topper Level)

### Example 1: Full 2D Unsteady Pathline Derivation (GATE Standard)
**Problem:** An unsteady velocity field is given by $u = 2t$ and $v = 3x$ (in $\text{m/s}$). A fluid particle is located at $(x_0, y_0) = (2, 1)$ at time $t_0 = 0$.
1. Derive the parametric position equations $(x(t), y(t))$ of the particle.
2. Find the exact cartesian equation $y = f(x)$ of the pathline.
3. Calculate the velocity vector $\vec{V}$ and acceleration vector $\vec{a}$ of the particle at $t = 2\text{ s}$.

> [!faq]- 🔍 View Solution & Detailed Explanation
> **1. Parametric Position Equations:**
> Integrate $u$:
> $$\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t \implies \int_2^x dx' = \int_0^t 2t' dt' \implies x - 2 = t^2 \implies \mathbf{x(t) = t^2 + 2}$$
> Integrate $v$:
> $$\frac{dy}{dt} = 3x = 3(t^2 + 2) = 3t^2 + 6$$
> $$\int_1^y dy' = \int_0^t (3t'^2 + 6) dt' \implies y - 1 = t^3 + 6t \implies \mathbf{y(t) = t^3 + 6t + 1}$$
> 
> ---
> **2. Cartesian Pathline Equation:**
> From $x(t)$: $t^2 = x - 2 \implies t = \sqrt{x - 2}$ (for $x \ge 2$).
> Substitute $t = \sqrt{x - 2}$ into $y(t)$:
> $$y = (\sqrt{x-2})^3 + 6\sqrt{x-2} + 1 = (x-2)\sqrt{x-2} + 6\sqrt{x-2} + 1$$
> $$\mathbf{y = (x + 4)\sqrt{x - 2} + 1 \quad (x \ge 2)}$$
> 
> ---
> **3. Particle Velocity and Acceleration at $t = 2\text{ s}$:**
> At $t = 2$:
> $x(2) = 2^2 + 2 = 6\text{ m}$
> $u(2) = 2(2) = 4\text{ m/s}$
> $v(2) = 3(6) = 18\text{ m/s}$
> $$\vec{V}(2) = 4\hat{i} + 18\hat{j}\text{ m/s}$$
> Magnitude: $\|\vec{V}\| = \sqrt{4^2 + 18^2} = \sqrt{16 + 324} = \sqrt{340} \approx \mathbf{18.44\text{ m/s}}$
> 
> Acceleration components:
> $$a_x = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 2 + (4)(0) + (18)(0) = 2\text{ m/s}^2$$
> $$a_y = \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0 + (4)(3) + (18)(0) = 12\text{ m/s}^2$$
> $$\vec{a}(2) = 2\hat{i} + 12\hat{j}\text{ m/s}^2$$
> Magnitude: $\|\vec{a}\| = \sqrt{2^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{4 + 144} = \sqrt{148} \approx \mathbf{12.17\text{ m/s}^2}$$

### Example 2: Transit Time and Pathline in a Decelerating Diffuser
**Problem:** In a 1D steady diffuser of length $L = 1.5\text{ m}$, air decelerates linearly from $u_1 = 60\text{ m/s}$ at the inlet ($x = 0$) to $u_2 = 20\text{ m/s}$ at the exit ($x = L$).
1. Formulate the velocity distribution $u(x)$.
2. Calculate the exact transit time of an air parcel traveling from inlet to exit.
3. Compare this with the naive transit time calculated using the simple arithmetic average speed.

> [!faq]- 🔍 View Solution & Detailed Explanation
> **1. Velocity Distribution:**
> $$u(x) = u_1 + \left(\frac{u_2 - u_1}{L}\right)x = 60 + \left(\frac{20 - 60}{1.5}\right)x = 60 - \frac{80}{3}x$$
> 
> **2. Exact Transit Time:**
> $$t_{\text{transit}} = \int_0^L \frac{dx}{u(x)} = \int_0^{1.5} \frac{dx}{60 - \frac{80}{3}x}$$
> Let $w = 60 - \frac{80}{3}x \implies dw = -\frac{80}{3}dx \implies dx = -\frac{3}{80}dw$.
> Limits: at $x = 0 \implies w = 60$; at $x = 1.5 \implies w = 20$.
> $$t_{\text{transit}} = -\frac{3}{80} \int_{60}^{20} \frac{dw}{w} = \frac{3}{80}\ln\left(\frac{60}{20}\right) = \frac{3}{80}\ln(3) = \frac{3}{80}(1.09861) \approx \mathbf{0.0412\text{ s} = 41.2\text{ ms}}$$
> 
> **3. Comparison with Naive Arithmetic Average:**
> $$u_{\text{avg}} = \frac{u_1 + u_2}{2} = \frac{60 + 20}{2} = 40\text{ m/s}$$
> $$t_{\text{naive}} = \frac{L}{u_{\text{avg}}} = \frac{1.5}{40} = 0.0375\text{ s} = 37.5\text{ ms}$$
> Percentage Error:
> $$\text{Error} = \frac{41.2 - 37.5}{41.2} \times 100\% \approx \mathbf{9.0\% \text{ underestimation!}}$$
> *Always use logarithmic integration for fluid transit time!*

### Example 3: Particle Stokes Number & Aircraft Icing Impingement (GATE AE High-Yield)
**Problem:** An aircraft flies at $U_0 = 120\text{ m/s}$ in icing atmospheric clouds containing supercooled water droplets of diameter $d_p = 40\,\mu\text{m}$ ($\rho_p = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$). The wing leading edge has chord $c = 2.0\text{ m}$ (effective nose curvature length $L = 0.1\text{ m}$). Air viscosity is $\mu = 1.8\times 10^{-5}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$.
1. Calculate the droplet relaxation time $\tau_p$.
2. Compute the droplet Stokes number $Stk$.
3. Determine whether the droplets follow the curving airflow around the wing or impinge directly onto the leading edge.

> [!faq]- 🔍 View Solution & Detailed Explanation
> **1. Droplet Relaxation Time ($\tau_p$):**
> $$\tau_p = \frac{\rho_p d_p^2}{18\mu}$$
> Given $d_p = 40\times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$, $d_p^2 = 1.6\times 10^{-9}\text{ m}^2$:
> $$\tau_p = \frac{(1000)(1.6\times 10^{-9})}{18(1.8\times 10^{-5})} = \frac{1.6\times 10^{-6}}{3.24\times 10^{-4}} \approx \mathbf{4.938\times 10^{-3}\text{ s} \approx 4.94\text{ ms}}$$
> 
> **2. Stokes Number ($Stk$):**
> Flow timescale around the leading edge nose:
> $$\tau_f = \frac{L}{U_0} = \frac{0.1\text{ m}}{120\text{ m/s}} = 8.333\times 10^{-4}\text{ s} \approx 0.833\text{ ms}$$
> Stokes number:
> $$Stk = \frac{\tau_p}{\tau_f} = \frac{4.938\times 10^{-3}}{8.333\times 10^{-4}} = \mathbf{5.93}$$
> 
> **3. Physical Consequence:**
> Since $Stk = 5.93 \gg 1.0$, the water droplets have **high inertia** and cannot negotiate the sharp streamline curvature around the leading edge.
> **Conclusion:** The droplets cross the fluid streamlines and **impinge directly onto the wing leading edge**, forming severe ice accretion that degrades aerodynamic lift.

## ⚠️ Common Exam Traps & Pitfalls (Where Marks are Lost)

> 🔴 **Trap 1: Confusing Initial Time $t_0$ in Pathline Integration**
> - *Mistake:* Assuming $t_0 = 0$ for all particles.
> - *Correction:* A particle released at $t = t_0$ must satisfy the lower integration limit $\int_{t_0}^t dt'$. If $t_0 \neq 0$, substituting $t_0 = 0$ shifts the entire trajectory in space and time.

> 🔴 **Trap 2: Calculating Transit Time via Arithmetic Average Speed**
> - *Mistake:* Using $t = L / \frac{u_1 + u_2}{2}$ in accelerating or decelerating nozzles.
> - *Correction:* Velocity is a function of position $u(x)$. The exact transit time MUST be computed as $t = \int_0^L \frac{dx}{u(x)}$.

> 🔴 **Trap 3: Assuming PIV Directly Measures Pathlines**
> - *Mistake:* Confusing PIV (Particle Image Velocimetry) with PTV (Particle Tracking Velocimetry).
> - *Correction:* **PIV** measures instantaneous Eulerian velocity vectors $\vec{V}(x,y)$ (yielding **Streamlines**). **PTV** tracks individual particle histories over time (yielding **Pathlines**).

> 🔴 **Trap 4: Missing the Square in Stokes Number Diameter Scaling**
> - *Mistake:* Assuming relaxation time $\tau_p \propto d_p$.
> - *Correction:* $\tau_p = \frac{\rho_p d_p^2}{18\mu} \propto \mathbf{d_p^2}$. Doubling the droplet diameter increases inertia and Stokes number by **4 times**!
